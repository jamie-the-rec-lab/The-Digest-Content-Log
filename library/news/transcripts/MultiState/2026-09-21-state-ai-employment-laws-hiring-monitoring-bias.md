## Why this matters to a recruiter

This is a direct regulatory hit on the automated hiring tools this digest covers every week. States considered "155 bills addressing AI in the workplace during the 2026 legislative session," and two states actually enacted hiring-specific rules: Colorado and Connecticut now require employers using automated hiring tools to notify candidates and explain adverse decisions, with a right to request human review. California went further and separately banned AI tools from reading workers' emotional state or neural data, though that bill (AB 1883) still needs Governor Newsom's signature and he has vetoed a similar bill before. For any team running AI screening or interview scoring, "notify and explain" is quickly becoming a baseline legal requirement, not a nice-to-have.

## Key figures

- "Colorado enacted [CO SB 189], which would require deployers of a covered automatic decision making tool to notify applicants of its use and to provide a detailed explanation following an adverse outcome."
- Connecticut SB 5 "mandates employer notification to employees and applicants when automated decision-making tools are used, with explanations of adverse decisions and data correction opportunities."
- "States considered 155 bills addressing AI in the workplace during the 2026 legislative session, with proposals focused on transparency, accountability, worker privacy, and workforce protection."
- Bias/impact-assessment mandates did not pass anywhere this session: "Multiple states...proposed legislation requiring employers to conduct bias or impact assessments on AI systems used in employment decisions, but all of these bills died in committee."
- California AB 1883 would "limit businesses' ability to use artificial intelligence tools to monitor workers' nervous systems and emotional states," sent to the Governor, with civil penalties "up to $500" per violation if signed (Bloomberg Law, 2026-08-31). Newsom "previously vetoed a broader 2025 bill on AI in personnel decisions," so signature is not guaranteed.
- Also flagged in the same roundup: "California sent legislation ([CA AB 1883])... Illinois limits AI-analyzed video interviews in hiring, and Maryland restricts the use of facial recognition technology" (existing law context from the Bloomberg piece).

## Full article

MultiState's 21 September 2026 roundup of the 2026 state legislative session found that AI-in-the-workplace was one of the most active areas of state lawmaking, with 155 bills introduced addressing transparency, accountability, worker privacy and workforce protection. Only two states actually enacted hiring-specific automated-decision-tool laws this session: Colorado (SB 189) and Connecticut (SB 5). Both require employers deploying automated hiring/decision tools to notify applicants that such a tool is in use, and to give a detailed explanation when the tool contributes to an adverse decision; both also grant applicants a route to correct inaccurate data feeding the tool and to request human review of the outcome. Neither MultiState's piece nor the corroborating Bloomberg Law article gives an effective date for either statute.

Separately, California's legislature passed AB 1883, which targets AI workplace surveillance of employees' emotional state and neural data rather than hiring decisions specifically; it was sent to Governor Newsom's desk and, per Bloomberg Law (31 August 2026), would let the state labor commissioner or a public prosecutor bring enforcement action, with civil penalties of up to $500 per violation if it becomes law. Newsom has vetoed a broader AI-in-personnel-decisions bill before, so its fate is not yet settled. Bloomberg Law's piece also notes existing state rules in the same space: Illinois already limits AI-analyzed video interviews in hiring, and Maryland already restricts employer use of facial recognition technology.

MultiState reports that a stronger requirement, mandatory bias or impact assessments on AI systems used in employment decisions, was proposed in multiple states this session but did not survive: every such bill died in committee. That leaves "notify and explain," rather than "prove it's unbiased," as this session's actual legal bar for automated hiring tools in the states that acted.

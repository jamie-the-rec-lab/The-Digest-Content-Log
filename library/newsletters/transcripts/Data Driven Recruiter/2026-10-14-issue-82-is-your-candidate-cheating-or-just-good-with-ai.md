Issue 82 - Is Your Candidate Cheating, Or Just Good With AI??

We tell candidates they can't use AI in the interview. Then the Monday they start, we tell them we are AI native. A recruitment agency owner in Manchester told the Guardian this week that you can spot candidates using AI because "It's like they are reading off an Autocue". Meanwhile, next week in Vegas, Goodfit will launch an AI Fluency Test at HR Tech to see how well candidates work with AI. So which is it: cheating, or a core skill?

### 1. How much cheating is actually going on?

The Institute of Student Employers' Student Recruitment Survey 2026: 92% of employers suspected or caught misconduct, up from 85% last year and 78% the year before. 66% caught at least one grad using AI in an interview. There are 141 applications for every graduate role.

22% of US job seekers in a Resume Genius survey admitted using AI live in interviews. Fabric looked at 19,368 AI-run interviews and flagged 38.5% of candidates; that rose to 48% in software engineering, compared with 12% in sales. Bear in mind Fabric sells cheating-detection software, so they might be motivated to find cheaters. In Robert Half's survey, 38% of HR leaders said they'd added extra interviews to verify skills.

### 2. Meanwhile, in Silicon Valley

Meta started running an AI-assisted coding round in October 2025, replacing one of its old algorithm rounds. Canva swapped its CS fundamentals interview for AI-assisted coding in June 2025. Google has been piloting something similar for junior and mid-level engineers. Canva's reasoning: candidates were using AI anyway and covert use was hard to spot; almost half its engineers were using AI coding tools every day. Amazon told recruiters that candidates may be disqualified for using AI in interviews.

### 3. Where's the line?

An arbitrary line the author made up:
- Disclosure: AI fluency is declared or allowed by your rules; AI misuse is hidden.
- Who's driving: fluency is the candidate directing, checking and editing; misuse is the candidate reading the answer out.
- Explaining it: fluency means they can defend every line; misuse falls apart at the second "why?"
- Experience: fluency sharpens real stories; misuse makes up stories that never happened.
- Stage: fluency is where you said AI is fine; misuse is where you said it isn't.

Last year 45% of employers gave candidates no guidance on AI at all. This year 82% publish guidance.

### 4. Is the line moving faster than we can keep up?

Short answer, yes. Fabric's cheating flags jumped from 9% to 45% in three months of late 2025. Most of the 82% of employer policies didn't exist a year ago. Treat your AI interview policy like your comp bands: write it down and review it every quarter.

### 5. How to run an AI-positive interview

1. Tell them the rules up front, in the interview invite: which stages AI is fine for, which it isn't, and why.
2. Split the process: one conversation about real experience with tools closed, and one working session where AI is open.
3. Hit pause: mid-interview, say "Take two minutes, use whatever tool you like, then talk me through what you'd keep and what you'd bin."
4. Mark the edit, not the output. Ask what the AI got wrong. Canva treats blindly accepting AI output as a red flag.
5. Go three levels deep on one story: who was in the room, what broke, what they'd do differently.
6. Ask about their actual AI workflow without relying on previous experience. "Show me something you built with AI last month" is not a good question because it limits the candidate to their current company's AI fluency. A better one: "Think about something that you'd love to improve with AI right now. Imagine I gave you 4 hours a week and an infinite tooling budget. What would you improve, and how do you go about improving it?"
7. Reset before you reject. Nervous people pause, read notes and sound rehearsed, and some very well prepared neurodivergent candidates can also sound rehearsed.
8. Practise what you preach. If you use AI to screen or score candidates, tell them. 57% of candidates think companies should have to disclose when AI is judging them.

### For your interview invites

"We use AI every day here, and we expect you will too. In this process, the [stage name] is AI-open: use whatever tools help you, and we'll ask you to talk us through your choices. The [stage name] is a conversation about your experience, so please leave the tools closed for that one. If you're unsure, ask. We won't hold it against you."

I don't think anyone has properly cracked this yet, me included. Banning AI outright feels daft when we expect people to use it from day one. A free-for-all with no rules tells you nothing. Clear rules plus one AI-open stage is the best I've got for now.

The author started investigating when candidates emailed his team about Ava Labs interviews the team had never heard of, run by recruiters they had never hired. Candidates described a friendly recruiter on LinkedIn, a couple of chat rounds, then a video interview link where a box said their video driver was broken, with a button to fix it. "The button installed malware."

### 1. What I found down the rabbit hole
"The driver trick has a name. Security researchers call it ClickFake Interview. Sekoia linked it to North Korea's Lazarus Group and found 184 fake job sites running it." It targets crypto, and engineers, marketers, finance and BD people have been caught. The engineer version is a take-home coding test with malware hidden inside (one version hid it inside SVG pictures of country flags). Long cons run several interviews, a fake assessment platform, an offer letter, an onboarding portal, then a request for bank details, ID and tax info; some send a cheque for home-office kit that later bounces.
"Job scam losses reported to the FBI went from $70m in 2023 to $363m in 2025. Here in the UK, job scam reports to Action Fraud more than doubled between 2022 and 2024." The other way: "Gartner surveyed 3,000 candidates and 6% admitted to interview fraud... Palo Alto Networks found someone with no technical skills could build a deepfake candidate in about 70 minutes."

### 2. Recruiter scams: what to look out for
Candidates asking about roles you don't recognise; LinkedIn profiles claiming to be in your team; job ads on sites you don't post to; lookalike email domains; candidates mentioning tools you don't use.

### 3. Candidate scams: what to look out for
Lips out of sync with voice; reluctance to turn the camera on; word-perfect answers after a strange pause; work history you can't confirm; references reachable only on Telegram or WhatsApp; work laptop to be sent to a different address from the one on their ID.

### 4. What you can actually do
Publish your real recruiters. (Names, LinkedIn links, email domain, tools); sweep LinkedIn monthly ("If your company page is verified, admins can now remove people falsely claiming to work there"); give candidates a one-page safety doc; log every report (date, platform, fake profile or domain, role, how far the candidate got); make sure your own process doesn't look like a scam (no zip-file take-homes, no unknown tools, no bank details before the offer is signed); ID check before offer and again on day one, confirm history with the employer directly, laptop to the address on their ID.

### 5. Candidate safety doc contents
Real recruiters with profile links; the only email domain used; interview and test tools; never asked to install software or paste a command to join an interview; never asked to pay or deposit a cheque; bank details and ID only after a signed offer through the onboarding system; how to contact you; where to report a scam (UK: Action Fraud, Police Scotland; US: ic3.gov).

### TL;DR
"Scammers are pretending to be your recruiters, and some of your candidates might not be who they say they are either." Make it easier for candidates to spot a fake and harder for a fake to get through.

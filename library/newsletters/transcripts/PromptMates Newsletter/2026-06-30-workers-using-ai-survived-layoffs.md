## Build of the Week

### How to Set Up a Claude Code Workspace That Keeps Every Recruiting Project in Context

Dan, a recruiting and people ops practitioner, shared the folder architecture he uses with Claude Code to keep context sharp across 40+ active projects, from sourcing tools to exec talent tracking.

The problem is common: sessions that start from scratch and context that bleeds between unrelated work. This structure eliminates both.

**Step 1: Create a ~/claude-brain/ Root and Add Domain Subfolders**

Keep a top-level directory for your work context: team notes, impact logs, and people profiles. Everything below it is a project. The folder you are in scopes what Claude knows.

**Step 2: Create One Subfolder Per Build or Project**

Each recruiting build gets its own subdirectory. When you move into that folder, Claude focuses only on that project. Context from a sourcing tool does not bleed into intake automation.

**Step 3: Write Your CLAUDE.md Once and Keep It Current**

Place a CLAUDE.md file in ~/.claude/. Claude reads it before every session. Put your role, tools, rules, and current priorities there. You never have to re-introduce yourself or explain what you are building.

Install MCP servers at the project level, not globally. If a sourcing MCP only applies to one build, configure it inside that project folder. Fewer global tools means fewer tokens consumed on every message.

Working from ~/claude-brain/ puts Claude in general work mode with your full context. Dropping into a project folder narrows focus to that build. No extra prompting required to switch - the workspace handles it.

## Worth Seeing & Reading

Gallup research finds workers who use AI at least monthly had a 6% chance of being laid off, compared to 18% for those using it less. Bonnie Dilber reframes the narrative: this is workers using AI replacing workers who are not, not AI replacing workers. Companies are now openly sharing AI fluency rubrics in job postings and building AI usage into comp plans.

Joe Atkinson argues most AI enablement programs are too generic to shift behavior. Knowing how Claude works is a starting point. Knowing how to apply it to specific recruiting scenarios is what makes a difference. He draws the parallel: you'd rather learn from a practitioner in your field than a course built for everyone. The same logic applies to AI training programs inside organizations.

At SHRM26, Simon Sinek and BambooHR CEO Brad Rencher argued that AI adoption is not the real challenge. The harder problem is redesigning work around AI with clear guidance, employee trust, and honest communication. Rencher said 73% of companies now use AI, while 58% have not provided real guidance. Sinek warned that vague replacement narratives create anxiety, and that leaders lose credibility when they answer real concerns with "toxic positivity."

Bain argues that AI advantage now depends less on tool access and more on operating model redesign. Companies pulling ahead have stronger data foundations, governance, role design, and leadership ownership. For TA, the lesson is direct: AI recruiting tools cannot fix fragmented scorecards, inconsistent feedback, stale ATS data, or unclear decision rights. They only expose them faster.

## Framework of the Week: The Other-Person Interview Framework

Laszlo Bock argues that interviews are not just tests of qualification. They are social conversations.

The candidates who perform best understand that the interview is not really about them. It is about the person across the table and what that person needs to believe before making a hiring decision.

Before an interview, ask:

- What does this interviewer care about?
- What problem is this team trying to solve?
- What risk are they trying to reduce?
- What evidence would make me feel safe to hire this person?
- Where does the candidate need to reframe their experience?
- What would make this conversation memorable after five other interviews?

Most candidates prepare to prove they can do the job. That is necessary, but incomplete. The stronger move is to make the interviewer's decision easier by connecting experience to the team's current problem.

The key insight: qualifications get a candidate into the conversation. Positioning determines whether the interviewer knows where to place them.

## Big Idea: Entry-Level Hiring Is Becoming AI Supervision

The old entry-level job was built around doing the first layer of work. The new entry-level job is moving toward checking, guiding, and improving AI-assisted work.

A new Cognizant and Pearson study found that 94% of HR leaders expect AI to create new entry-level roles within five years. Even more telling: 96% expect entry-level roles to evolve into positions where employees supervise or manage AI systems.

That changes what TA teams should screen for.

The question is no longer only whether a candidate can complete the task. It is whether they can judge the output, spot mistakes, ask better follow-up questions, and know when a human needs to intervene.

The training gap is already visible. Six in ten HR leaders say their learning and development programs are not keeping pace with how quickly AI is reshaping entry-level work.

For TA, this means early-career hiring needs a new scorecard. Degrees and task experience matter less if the role itself is becoming AI oversight. Adaptability, problem-solving, communication, and judgment become the signals to measure.

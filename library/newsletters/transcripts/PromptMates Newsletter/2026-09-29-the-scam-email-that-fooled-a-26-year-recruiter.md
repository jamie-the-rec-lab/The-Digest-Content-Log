## Build of the week: Build a Flatter Team Without Removing Managers

Flat organisations are usually framed as having fewer managers. Mind Robotics' Brandon Sammut argues that the org chart matters less than how quickly information, context, decisions and recognition move through the company. His team uses four questions to test whether they're actually operating flat.

1. Who has the information? Make standups, decisions, notes and next steps public by default unless they're confidential. If someone needs the right relationship or a chain of 1:1s just to understand why a decision was made, information isn't moving freely enough.
2. How does context travel? Let people go directly to whoever has the best context instead of routing everything through their manager. The goal is to shorten the path between the person with the question and the person who can answer it.
3. Who decides? Give every body of work one named decision-maker from the start. Flat shouldn't mean consensus on everything. Clear ownership keeps fewer layers from turning into more confusion.
4. Who gets credit? Push recognition toward the people closest to the work, then make sharing what they learned part of the responsibility. Reward delivery, alignment and contribution rather than title, level or team size.

The takeaway: you don't build a flat organisation by deleting boxes from the org chart. You build one by removing the places where information, context, decisions and credit get stuck.

## Big idea

A 26-year recruiting veteran almost missed a scam job offer this week, and the only thing that caught it was reading a sender's email domain closely.

"Real recruiting outreach and scam outreach look nearly identical now, and that is a message problem talent teams created themselves."

Last week, Keirsten Greggs received an email about an internal talent acquisition role at Apple. The recruiter's LinkedIn profile checked out completely: real name, real title, real tenure at Apple, and connections to TA leaders she knew personally.

Apple's careers site assigns every job posting a searchable role number, but the email never named a role. What exposed the scam was something smaller.

Apple calls its HR function the People Team, not HR. The sender line read Careers HR, and the domain didn't match Apple's.

The recruiter whose identity was used was a victim too.

Greggs had already published a list of fraud red flags in March. Domain mismatch topped that list, and it held. But her advice to verify recruiters on LinkedIn failed this test. The person was real. The outreach wasn't.

No role number, no team, no level, a no-reply address and a booking link instead of a reply. Each was a warning sign, but none was conclusive on its own. Legitimate recruiting outreach increasingly looks much the same.

Open the last outreach message you sent a candidate. What's the one detail they could independently verify to prove it came from you? If there isn't one, that's the work.

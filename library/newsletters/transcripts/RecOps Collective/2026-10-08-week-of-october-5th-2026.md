## Newsletter

Lead piece: Incorporating Solutions Engineering Principles into Your Tool Evaluation, by Jeremy Lyons.

The author says he is not passing judgment; every situation is different and the intent is knowledge sharing. While not formally trained as a solutions engineer, he is often put in situations where he needs to demonstrate that skill set for prospective and current clients. Because other RecOps and TA Ops consultants often ask him the questions he asks, he shares them, since some may apply to in-house teams.

Questions he asks:
- Why are we making the change? People change tools for many reasons. He listens for the pain point. The fascinating cases are when there is no pain point but people want a change, either individual ("I want to switch to the new tool everyone else is using") or cultural ("We like to experiment so it isn't uncommon for us to change every year"). Sometimes people just want validation and need to hear it out loud.
- If you choose XYZ, are you prepared for ABC? An overlooked element of a tool is the culture change that comes with it. More tools are pivoting to per-seat or usage models versus company-size or general permission models. If the pivot creates operations antithetical to the company's value proposition, positioning and enablement need to help people adopt it. Great tools fail simply because they required a different approach.
- Is there time to learn? Jason Miller pointed out that tool onboarding is getting more complicated, not less. Two things to keep in mind: people want to do more than they want to learn (AI changes this because it does the learning and gives the output), and learning takes time, which is scarce. If you recommend a tool with a high upfront learning cost, account for it in the recommendation.
- Do you have time to learn? An internal version of the same question: when implementing a tool you have never used, you need the time and network to learn it so you can implement it correctly.
- Is a finished product needed, or is this a place to get experimental? "No one got fired for buying IBM" exists for a reason. Some companies only want established vendors; others want a vendor partnership where they grow together and give feedback.
- Do we need to buy this, or can we build it? If you can define the problem statement, you may find it doesn't need a vendor and has a simpler solution you can build (with the caveat that expertise and time are available).

To close: it is easy to refer your favourite tool in, but what is best for you might not be best for others, so balance your recommendations; this is where neutrality helps.

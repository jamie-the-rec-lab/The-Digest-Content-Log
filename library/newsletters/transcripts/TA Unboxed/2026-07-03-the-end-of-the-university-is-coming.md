# The End of the University is coming

- **Source:** TA Unboxed
- **Date:** 2026-07-03
- **Fetched:** 2026-07-06

## Newsletter

Here's my big headline

Universities, in their current mass-market form, are heading for a structural break.

This is my personal opinion, but I sense this will play out over the next 3-5 years.

Why? Because the university bundle was a product designed for a world where:
knowledge was scarce,
credentials were trusted,
environments were hard to replicate,
and careers moved slowly.

AI is starting to break those assumptions at speed.

If you are an employer, builder, or founder, the question you'll be asking is:
Where will capable people come from?
How will you know they are capable?
Also, how do you build capability inside a company when "education" no longer arrives pre-packaged as a degree?

**1. The university is not one product. It is four.**

The mistake in most "education disruption" takes is treating the university as a single thing.

It is not.

It is a four-part bundle:
Access to knowledge
Access to credential
Access to the environment (which is really access to a network)
Focused Time — three to four years, structured, away from the workforce

That bundle made perfect sense when the world was stable enough that you could lock someone into a three-to-four-year cycle, and the outputs would still be relevant at the end.

AI is unbundling the first two; economics is exposing the third; and the fourth is being disrupted at scale, at the breakneck speed at which things are changing.

Metaview recently announced something special.

fillmore: an autonomous sourcing agent. You tell it who you need. It researches, contacts, and schedules the most relevant candidates for the role, 24/7, without you driving each step.

fillmore is pitched at the next thing along. It doesn't just find people. It acts.

Three moves, end to end:
Research the role and the market, and build a picture of who fits.
Reach out with tailored messages to the people who match.
Schedules the ones who say yes, straight into your calendar.

The biggest shift in sourcing right now. Most sourcing builds a pipeline that you then have to warm up, chase, and convert. fillmore flips that round.

It books you a pipeline of candidates who already want to speak to you.

**2. Pillar one collapses first: knowledge is no longer scarce**

Universities used to be a scarce interface to knowledge:
libraries,
professors,
curated curriculum,
a structured sequence.

Now, knowledge distribution has been commoditised. The internet did the first wave. AI is doing the second.

The key shift is not "people can Google answers."

It is that AI creates a new learning interface:
it tutors,
it adapts,
it quizzes,
it explains in multiple ways,
it collapses friction and time,
it can stay with you at the exact moment you get stuck.

Tools like YouLearn and Remnote exist specifically to turn learning materials into notes, interactive chats, quizzes, and "AI tutor" workflows.

The world and technology are evolving rapidly. A three-year course cannot keep pace with the fast-changing market.

What this means for employers

Most hiring pipelines still assume:
People arrive trained, and employers select from pre-qualified pools.

That assumption will decay.

The new scarcity is not information. It is:
judgment,
critical thinking,
deliberate practice,
real-world application,
feedback loops,
and the ability to turn learning into shipped output.

In other words: capability.

But capability is not produced by content. It is produced by reps, who aren't specialised in universities.

**3. Pillar two is next: the credential is losing signal power**

Degrees have been a trust shortcut. Not perfect, but useful:
"Someone else filtered. This candidate probably has baseline competence."

The problem is that the degree signal is increasingly misaligned with how value is created now.

A degree is a slow credential in a fast world. Three to four years is an eternity in industries where we're seeing toolchains change annually, workflows change quarterly, and entire job categories can appear and disappear in a year.

For builders and founders, this is the real issue: you are hiring for a moving target, using a credential produced on a fixed timetable.

Most people studying often move into careers outside their field, which seems illogical. Why? Because an 18-year-old typically has little idea of what they want to do without experiencing any work.

It opens up a deeper question about what comes next and who gets to be trusted.

That question is the subject of Part 2.

**4. Pillar three: the environment — the last pillar standing, and priced like a luxury**

The university environment matters.

Cohorts matter. Identity formation matters. Networks matter. Leaving home matters.

The social experience is one of the selling points, not the biggest (I know it was definitely for me). To be a student again.

But it is also the most expensive part of the bundle, and it is the least defensible when:
costs keep rising,
debt becomes generational,
and the opportunity cost of delaying real-world reps continues to increase.

So the question becomes brutally simple:
Is the environment worth the price tag and the runway cost?

For an increasing percentage of people, it will not be.

The environment will not disappear. It will migrate.

The replacement is not "learning alone."

It is a new stack and I'll share more on what I think will take over in part 3 of the series.

**5. Pillar four: time — the assumption nobody questions**

Three years. One subject. Away from the workforce.

That model was designed for a world where knowledge was stable, careers were linear, and the skills you needed at 21 were roughly the same ones you would need at 35. None of those things is true anymore.

We have built an entire assumption into the education system that time spent in study is equivalent to compounding capability. That's been seriously challenged.

Three years studying marketing is not the same as three years doing marketing.

Three years of reading about leadership is not the same as three years of making decisions under pressure. The knowledge might transfer. The capability does not.

In practice, here is what those three years often look like: year one is adjustment and a broad survey. Year two is engagement. Year three is one narrow dissertation and preparation to leave. Then the graduate starts a job and learns the actual work from scratch.

The tools for learning faster, building real things sooner, and proving capability earlier have never been better.

If you think about where we're heading, just bear with me. If you think about it, three years in one subject, producing one certificate, with no real output and no market feedback? That doesn't seem right.

**The conclusion for Part 1**

The university bundle is being pulled apart at every seam:
Knowledge — commoditised by AI.
Credentials — losing signal power in a fast-moving world.
Environment — real, but increasingly mispriced. The network it produces is the valuable part, and that can be built without the institution.
Focused Time — three years in one subject is not three years of compounding capability. The assumption is wrong, and the costs are rising.

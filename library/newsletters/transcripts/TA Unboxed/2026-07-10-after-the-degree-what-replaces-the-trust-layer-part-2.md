# After the Degree: What Replaces the Trust Layer? (Part 2)

- **Source:** TA Unboxed
- **Date:** 2026-07-10
- **Fetched:** 2026-07-11

## Newsletter

The employers who win will stop filtering for credentials and start filtering for proof.

Say you're reviewing a candidate who looks perfect on paper. Good degree. Clean CV. Polished answers. The work samples look sharp, probably because AI helped produce them. Then the job gets real. An ambiguous problem. Conflicting goals. A stakeholder who keeps moving the target. And the same candidate freezes. That gap, between looking capable and being capable, is the whole story of what happens next.

In Part 1, we pulled apart the university bundle: knowledge, credentials, environment, and time. AI commoditises the knowledge. Economics exposes the environmental impact and the three- to four-year time cost of being away from the workforce. That leaves the credential. This is about what happens when the credential stops working.

### 1. The credential isn't just slow. It's getting noisy.

A degree worked as a shortcut because it answered one question: "Has someone else already filtered this person?" Imperfect, but good enough. The alternative, building your own assessment, was expensive and hard.

That calculation is changing, because a degree no longer tells you what you actually need to know:

- Can they set direction and spot what's missing?
- Can they evaluate quality and decide with incomplete information?
- Can they think under pressure and learn fast when the ground shifts?
- Can they use AI without outsourcing their judgment?
- Can they take responsibility for an outcome, and actually finish it?

Strip it back and you can see what employers are really hiring for: learnability, coachability, adaptability, and clear thinking. None of those is a knowledge skill. None is reliably signalled by a degree. In an AI world they matter more, not less. When the tools change every few months, the person who learns fast and thinks clearly beats the person who simply arrives with a fixed body of knowledge.

### 2. AI makes it worse, because it fakes competence.

Here's the issue employers currently face when assessing candidates in the new world. AI makes it easy to appear competent, which is not the same as being competent. Don't offload the skill to the AI. Learn it. Most people will take the shortcut: ask, get an answer, feel smart, move on. That produces familiarity, not capability.

So the old filter (the degree) is weakening at the exact moment a new source of false signal (AI-polished output) is arriving. You will meet more candidates who present well, produce decent-looking work, and still fail when conditions get real and require high accountability. AI can draft. It can't take responsibility. So the real skill becomes the ability to judge quality and act under uncertainty, and that is very hard to read off a CV.

### 3. Where the trust goes next.

When the degree loses its default status, trust doesn't vanish. It relocates. And the replacement layer is already building itself:

- **Portfolios of shipped work**, proof that someone can produce, not just study
- **Work sample tests** that simulate the actual job, not a proxy for it
- **Skills-based assessments** that measure what matters
- **Reputation inside communities**, visible to people who know the work
- **Apprenticeship outcomes**, performance shown over time

AI speeds this up because it speeds up proof. If someone can build a prototype, write a clear memo, or shape a customer narrative in days rather than months, the gap between credentialed and non-credentialed paths collapses. The barrier to demonstrating capability is dropping, which means the barrier to exposing a lack of it is dropping too.

The likely outcome isn't one clean replacement. It's a messy middle: elite institutions keep their network and signalling power; research universities stay relevant at the frontier; mid-tier institutions get squeezed hardest, offering neither prestige nor proof; new players show up where the pain is worst: entry-level hiring and reskilling. Trust fragments first, then reconsolidates around whoever builds the most credible proof.

### 4. What employers should do now.

If degrees become a weaker filter, you have two options. Complain that candidates are worse, or build a better system for producing and spotting capability. Only the second one has a future.

**Shift from credential filtering to proof filtering.** Work sample tests that simulate the job. Paid trial periods where output is the measure. Structured interviews that test judgment, not recall. Clear internal standards for what "excellent at this level" actually looks like. This is more honest than degree filtering, and more defensible as AI makes output easy to fake.

**Stop thinking L&D content. Start thinking about reps and feedback.** Most corporate training fails because it's built like school: content delivered, notes taken, quiz passed, forgotten. Skills are built through repetition, pressure, feedback, and application. AI makes this far easier to scale. It can generate practice scenarios, role-play objections, and critique drafts. But only if you keep the discipline: AI is the sparring partner, not the performer.

**Reward capability that compounds, not tenure that accumulates.** The people who become force multipliers have clearer communication, better judgment, faster learning, and stronger systems thinking. Career ladders built on time served won't attract or keep them.

Underneath all of it, ask any candidate or team a simpler question: what reps are they running, what do they ship, and who verifies it? Capability beats curriculum in a fast world.

### 5. The villain, and the new questions.

If there's a clean villain in this story, it isn't universities. It's credentialism: the belief that an institution's stamp matters more than demonstrated capability, and the ecosystem that profits from keeping that belief alive.

University isn't useless. It's over-claimed. It still earns its place as a place for ambitious people to find each other, a cohort and identity engine, a network amplifier, and a research institution. The problem is treating it as the default, especially now that better alternatives exist for specific goals.

The old question was: "Where did you study?" The new questions are: How quickly can you learn? What have you shipped? How do you think under pressure? Can you use AI without losing your judgment?

AI didn't end education. It changed the questions. The organisations that win will build capability faster, verify it more reliably, and create places where talent compounds rather than stagnates. That's not a hiring problem. It's a systems problem. And systems problems have systems solutions.

### 6. The other side of the ledger: not just a weak signal, a bad deal

There's one more axis worth putting on the table, and it's the one that turns a hiring problem into a personal one. The degree isn't just a weak signal to employers. For the candidate and the parent paying for it, it's starting to look like a badly sold financial product: priced high, with terms that can move after an 18-year-old has signed.

Simon Squibb's documentary, *If You're Thinking About Going To University, Watch This*, makes this case bluntly, and the numbers hold up:

- **The competition has never been worse.** The Institute of Student Employers reported 140 applications per graduate vacancy in 2024, up 59% in a single year, the highest since records began in 1991.
- **The terms moved after people signed.** Outstanding student loan debt in England hit £267 billion in March 2025, forecast to reach around £500 billion by the late 2040s.
- **The first job is disappearing.** Graduate hiring fell around 8% from 2024 to 2025, and entry-level postings in the US are down roughly 35% since early 2023.

### Where this leaves us

The degree is a weakening default signal. It no longer tells you what you most need to know. AI makes that worse by faking competence, so proof now matters more than polish. Trust doesn't disappear. It is reflected in portfolios, work samples, reputation, and shipped work. It's not just a weak signal; in some European countries, it's a rising-cost, badly-sold product, sold into a market that's quietly removing the bottom rung. The employers who win will filter for proof, build reps and feedback, and reward compounding capability.

The short version: stop asking where someone studied. Start asking what they can do, and how you would know.

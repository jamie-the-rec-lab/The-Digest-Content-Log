# Universities Have 3 Years. Here's What Replaces Them.

## Newsletter

Part 1: the university bundle (knowledge, credentials, environment, and time) is being pulled apart. Part 2: trust is moving to proof of capability; the degree is a poorly sold, rising-cost product; and the time it consumes is an opportunity cost we rarely calculate honestly. Part 3 (this article) asks the question beneath it all: why do companies still recruit through universities at all, and what takes its place when the degree stops doing the job?

### Start with the real question

We usually ask whether university is worth it for the student. The more useful question for our world is why it is worth it for the employer. Companies do not recruit at universities out of habit or sentiment. They get specific things from it. Once you name them, you can see exactly what replaces each one.

### What a company is actually buying

When a business recruits at universities, it is really buying five things and none of them is about the subject studied.

A filter. The degree works as a screening signal. In signalling theory, a costly credential serves as a proxy for traits that an employer cannot observe directly: trainability, persistence, and baseline competence. The employer is not buying the knowledge. It is buying the sort.

Aggregation. Universities put tens of thousands of 18 to 21-year-olds in one place at one time. The milkround exists because campuses are cheap, dense places to recruit at scale. This is logistics, not learning.

A brand association. Recruiting from named institutions signals something back about the employer. It is employer branding as much as talent selection.

A maturity and reliability proxy. Three years of showing up, hitting deadlines, and finishing is read as evidence that someone can hold down a job. It signals persistence that someone can commit to something long and see it through. But persistence is not the same as capability.

A learnability proxy. The degree is also read as proof that someone can pick up information quickly, absorb new material, be trained, and get up to speed quickly. However, the assumption is that three years of study produces three years of compounding capability. It does not.

If what you are really buying is the ability to learn fast, a cohort that trains people on current tools and shows them learning in real time is a far sharper vehicle than a degree. All five are already weakening and all five can be sourced a better way.

### Why all five are weakening at once

The filter is losing trust. Almost two thirds of employers now use skills-based hiring to identify candidates, and for the third year running fewer than 40% screen by GPA. Around one in four employers said they would remove degree requirements for some roles by the end of 2025, and 70% of hiring managers say they now weigh relevant experience over a bachelor's degree. The LinkedIn Economic Graph's 2025 skills-based hiring report shows the trend accelerating across every major market.

As Part 3 sets out, AI is simultaneously thinning the entry-level rung that the degree filter used to feed into. The filter is losing trust at the exact moment the jobs behind it are changing shape.

### What replaces the campus: the cohort

So, a 3-4-year university degree isn't sustainable for me. This is my one prediction on where I think the model will change. To be clear, the university does not get reformed; it gets unbundled. I still see students attending and being on campus, but in a completely different format.

The three-to-five-year degree gives way to short, specialist cohorts: 4-6 months each, focused on one high-value field, such as digital marketing, web design, data analytics, sales, or operations. Two or three of them across a career, so roughly a year or 18 months in total. Chosen because they add value to a business from day one, not because they complete a syllabus. Built to feed people directly into a role, not as a detour before one.

The individual's job inside that model is simple and demanding: do the cohort, push to be the best in it, and show what you can truly do. The cohort serves as both the training and the proof.

### The cohort is the new aggregation point

This is the part that matters most for hiring. If the main reason companies tolerated the degree filter was that campuses aggregated talent cheaply, then cohorts do that job better. They aggregate people who are pre-sorted by field, trained on current tools, and ranked by demonstrated output. You recruit from the cohort the way you used to recruit from the campus, but with a sharper signal, without the three-year lag, and without the noise of an irrelevant subject mix.

### What changes when the cohort is AI-augmented

The cohort model changes further when you build AI into it from the start, not as a bolt-on or a productivity tip at the end of the programme, but as the practice partner throughout. It also lets you iterate on the material far faster. You can adjust the syllabus every six months, because the learning market now needs to adapt at that speed. A course about AI from three years ago is not the same as it is today. The outputs people can create with these tools are evolving so fast that the curriculum has to keep moving.

A marketing cohort where AI is integrated by design looks different from a marketing course that mentions AI. The gap between learning something and being able to ship something compresses dramatically. A learner can run real campaigns, produce professional-quality copy, and build a track record of actual output during the programme, not simulated projects, not hypotheticals.

The result is that what the employer receives from a well-run AI-augmented cohort is not potential. It is demonstrated output, produced faster and at a higher quality than previous cohort models could achieve. A graduate who has learned marketing with AI throughout their training knows how to use it purposefully, review and edit its output, and keep their own judgment in the loop. That integration is deeper than anything a degree curriculum delivers.

### The assessment that proves it

The most interesting development in this model is what assessment looks like when you take it seriously. Traditional cohort or course assessment is: did you pass the module, did you complete the project, did your instructor mark you as competent? That produces a certificate.

The AI-augmented cohort has an alternative: observable performance on a live project. One to two days near the end of the programme. The learner is given a real brief and has to deliver a defined output in a real working environment, a terminal, a design tool, a marketing platform. AI tools are available. Using AI well is part of what is being assessed. What gets measured is two things: the quality of what they ship, and how they used AI to get there, whether they prompted purposefully, iterated on weak outputs, and made good decisions when the model gave them something wrong. That is a far better signal than a grade. It is the closest proxy an employer has ever had for what they will actually get on day one.

### It already works where we have data

The cohort model is not theoretical. We have proxy evidence from the nearest equivalents. Someone completing a Level 5 apprenticeship can expect to earn around £1.5 million over a career, close to the average graduate, and around 72% of apprentices stay with their initial employer. Roughly 79% of coding bootcamp graduates are employed within 180 days, compared to around 68% of computer science graduates within a year, and they start earning sooner.

### What this means for TA practitioners

Stop thinking campus, start thinking cohort. The sourcing logic is the same, you want a dense pool of pre-sorted, trained candidates, but the signal from a cohort is substantially better. Here is what to do practically.

Build the pipeline before you have a vacancy. Identify two or three cohort providers in your key hiring fields and build a direct relationship now.

Redefine what strong looks like at entry level. Update your screening criteria to reflect what you actually need in the role.

Ask for the output, not the certificate. A good cohort produces work you can assess. Ask to see it.

Make the case internally. Hiring managers default to degrees because they are familiar and defensible. Your job is to give them a better shortcut: a cohort provider they trust, an assessment standard that holds, and enough early evidence that cohort hires perform.

The campus is not disappearing overnight. But the companies that start building cohort-to-role pipelines today will have a structural sourcing advantage within three years, as the degree filter continues to weaken and the competition for cohort graduates begins to look like the competition for campus ones does now.

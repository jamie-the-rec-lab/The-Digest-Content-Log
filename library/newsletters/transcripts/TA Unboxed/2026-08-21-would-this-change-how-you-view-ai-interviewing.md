## Newsletter

This week I want to explore an idea that’s been sitting in the back of my mind for a while. I’d love to know what you think.
It started with a spreadsheet
A while back, a senior TA leader did something most of us would never do.
They tracked every stage of their own job search. Every application. Every reply. Every silence.
The results were uncomfortable reading:
48% of applications got zero responses
Only 16% turned into interviews
LinkedIn’s EasyApply? A 0% success rate, flooding inboxes with so much volume that genuine candidates got buried
I’ve kept a record of those numbers since I first read them, as they’re indicative of what candidates have been telling us for years.
Applying for roles feels like applying into a void.
You see it in the LinkedIn comments every week. “The ATS rejected me.” “No human ever saw my CV.” I understand: when you don’t know what’s happening, people start to speculate; they don’t know if anyone was even there.
The recruitment industry has created a trust problem.

Now we’re adding AI to the interview process
There’s a lot of talk about AI interviewing right now, so let me be clear about where I stand.
It’s inevitable that more companies will embrace AI interviewing, and I personally welcome the shift. While some posts often focus on the drawbacks, there are significant benefits, and I hope to show you one today.
I actually expect that within a couple of years, AI interviewing will probably be the default first conversation in most hiring processes. It may replace the application or be required when you submit your CV/Resume.
Yes, there are still hurdles to overcome. Bias, accuracy, candidate anxiety, the human-in-the-loop and regulation, to name a few. But the version we see today won’t be the one we live with in 2028. Once people get used to AI in the consumer world, like booking plumbers, dealing with customer service or getting advice, it will start to feel normal in recruitment too.
We have to be careful, though. If we take a process that candidates already don’t trust and put a machine at the front of it, the void just gets bigger.
Today, I’m going to put a marker for companies to change how we think about AI interviewing. I call it the Candidate Trust Report.
The idea: a Candidate Trust Report
If a candidate applies and has an AI interview, what if, 24-48 hours later, they receive a report on their performance?
A structured, honest summary of what happened in that interview. How their answers landed. What happens next.
Not a score, but a review of their answers.
Here’s what it would include:
1. What we captured, and why
We didn’t score you. We captured what you shared and mapped it against what the role and team are looking for.
The criteria should be simple and up front. Problem-solving, communication, judgment under uncertainty, real examples. So you knew what we were listening for before you even started.
The report shows you the same picture the hiring team sees. The questions you were asked, the answers you gave, and how those answers lined up with each criterion.
No hidden measures. No mystery criteria you only find out about afterwards. If we were listening for it, you should be able to see it.
2. Results in bands, not scores
No fake star system or numbers that imply a certainty the AI doesn’t have.
Just three simple bands: Developing, Meets, or Strong across each dimension.
Developing means your answers didn’t yet give enough evidence for what this role needs. Meets means you showed what we were looking for. Strong means you went beyond it, with examples that stood out.
Bands keep everyone honest. A number like 7.4 out of 10 pretends to a precision that isn’t there. A band says what we actually know: roughly where your answers landed, and nothing more.
The challenge will be if you receive a strong score but no follow-up.
3. What you did well
Specific. Backed by evidence. Not generic praise.
“You showed strong structured thinking in question 2. You identified the constraints before proposing solutions.”
Each strength points to a moment in the interview. The question, what you said, and why it landed. So you can see exactly what worked and do more of it next time.
Candidates almost never get feedback like this. Even if you had an interview, you’re hearing it from a 3rd party, and it’s often quite generic.
Most people walk away from a rejection with nothing to work with. A lot of candidates don’t even get a rejection.
4. What to strengthen next time
The idea is that you’re providing something Practical. Forward-looking. Allow the candidate to work with the information they receive.
The framing matters. It’s not “you failed at this.” It’s “if you’re in this situation again, here’s what would make your answer stronger.”
So instead of “your examples lacked detail”, it reads more like: “In question 3, you described what the team delivered. Next time, walk us through your specific role and the decision you made. That’s what we’re listening for.”
Something a candidate can actually act on. Whether that’s with you or in their next interview somewhere else.
5. What this report is, and what it isn’t
This part has to be clear:
It explains some of the assessment signals from this step (you can’t give away too much otherwise, the system could be gamed)
It is not the final hiring decision
AI doesn’t know your full context: your prior work, your circumstances, anything you didn’t share
Final decisions involve multiple inputs, human in the loop and, where it matters, human decision if you made it.
This manages expectations on both sides. The candidate knows what the report can and can’t tell them, and the organisation has a clear record of what the AI assessed and where its limits were.
If a candidate received a strong candidate report but didn’t make it to the next round, companies will need to be clear on why.
6. A way to push back
Two links. That’s it.
→ If you think this assessment missed important context, request a review here.
→ If you need accommodations for future stages, ask for them here.
This is what turns a report into a right, rather than a nice gesture.
If a candidate believes the AI missed something, there has to be a route to a human. Without that, the report is just a nicer-looking rejection.
7. What happens next
If the next step is a conversation with the hiring manager, say when. If it’s a no, say that too, clearly and kindly.
It’s important to avoid a candidate trust report that gives overly positive feedback, only for the company to ghost the candidate later. I mentioned this last week; it could be related to shadow demand, but how do you communicate that to a candidate who’s invested time in preparing and interviewing for a role?
Why this matters more than it might seem
Look at what’s changing in this space:
Candidate fraud is on the rise.
Regulation is tightening. The EU AI Act already sets transparency obligations for AI in hiring.
Candidates are comparing notes. In forums, in communities, in reviews. The word gets out.
Trust is becoming a differentiator. When strong candidates have choices, how you treat them in the process is part of your offer.
The organisations that work out transparency now will be in a much better position when the pressure arrives.

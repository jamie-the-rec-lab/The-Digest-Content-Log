# When every application is AI-assisted, what are you actually evaluating?

- **Source:** TA Unboxed
- **Date:** 2026-09-04
- **Fetched:** 2026-09-10
- **Public archive:** https://taunboxed.substack.com/p/when-every-application-is-ai-assisted

## Core argument

Darren Bush opens with a real example from a colleague at Ericsson: she wrote a job advert, posted it, and got back a CV whose "experience" was described in the exact language of her own advert, word for word. He argues this is no longer an isolated case: "CVs and cover letters are being rewritten to echo the job advert's own words, and more numbers are being bolted onto every line, whether the metric is real or just there because it looks like the sort of thing that gets through a screen." Jason Miller reportedly compared CVs from the last 30 days against a stack from 2024 and found "every CV is getting longer. Every bullet sounds more impressive. Every candidate starts to read like the same candidate." John Vlastelica, quoted from a recent Recruiting Brainfood conversation, reframes the sourcing problem: the old metaphor was finding a needle in a haystack, but now it is "finding a needle in a stack of needles."

## Sponsor mention (Metaview)

Metaview has announced Screening, a voice-led AI screening agent. It runs "a real two-way conversation, not a static form, not a one-way video interview, and not an AI avatar," then hands recruiters an evaluated shortlist with scores, notes and context from the call. Bush states "the important bit: humans still make the decision", Screening does not auto-reject or auto-approve. The first 25 screens are offered free.

## The real issue

Bush's reframing: the question is no longer "did this person use AI," since a CV is now something "generated, polished and optimised by a tool" whenever the output is digital and asynchronous. The real question becomes whether the candidate can actually do the work, which a polished CV can no longer reliably signal.

## What to do now (checklist)

- Create and publish a candidate AI policy, not just an internal one. Bush cites Anthropic, Red Hat, Code.org and the UK Civil Service as organisations that already have public guidance on how candidates can and can't use AI in hiring.
- Audit the assessment process stage by stage (screening call, application form, take-home, technical test) and ask honestly whether AI could produce that output; if yes, "you're not learning what you think you're learning at that stage."
- Stop overvaluing polished applications: "a beautiful CV/Resume or cover letter is no longer strong evidence; it may just be good tool use."
- Move more assessment into live evidence: live problem-solving, structured follow-ups, work walkthroughs, discussion of trade-offs, things hard to fake in the moment.
- Ask candidates how they use AI as a capability signal rather than a trap: what they use it for, how they check its output, when they override it.
- Train hiring managers to spot an AI-polished answer, since the burden otherwise just moves one step down the process instead of being resolved.
- Decide what humans must still own: sorting, summarising and recommending can go to AI, but a named person must own the standard, fairness and the final call.
- Try AI tools purpose-built for this problem, citing Metaview's Screening as a concrete example of the pattern.

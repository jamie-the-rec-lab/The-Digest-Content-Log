## Newsletter

This idea has taken me 7 months to design, iterate and build. For as long as I can remember, I've been thinking about how we do hiring differently, including how we report on it. When you step back, hiring isn't linear, yet all our reports are linear by design. Recruiting doesn't happen perfectly, with everyone arriving at the same time at each stage. So I came to the conclusion: time in recruiting isn't fixed; it bends. A delay in one place can change what happens elsewhere. Feedback arrives late, the candidate's availability changes, and the panel slots you had pencilled in no longer work. The calendar still moves one day at a time; however, the work inside the req doesn't.

### The Hiring Clock

This led me to create what I call the Fluid Clock. There are different candidate journeys happening inside the same job. Picture a circular clock face, with sections for the stages of hiring, for example screening, interviewing, preboarding. Each shows progress against its expected duration. When a stage runs beyond that expectation, its section expands. Not because it has taken many days, but because it has taken longer than we agreed was reasonable for this job. The time expands to show how long you're spending on that stage relative to your SLA or agreed expectations for that role.

What goes around the clock face? The stages should reflect the process you're actually running. For a familiar hiring process you might start with: sourcing (where the role needs it, but this could be removed); advertising and applications; screening; shortlisting and hiring manager review; first interview; second interview; final interview, where required; offer and close.

The core insight: time in a hiring process does not flow at a constant rate. It bends. It expands in some stages and compresses in others, depending on candidate quality or hiring manager engagement. My ambition is to see a clock like this on every job. You open the job and, alongside the candidates and activity, there's its clock, showing where things are moving and where something needs attention. For those who read the last two newsletters, you'll see the quality signal and the 'one to watch'.

### The shape starts the conversation

One small image shows the stage a role has reached, where time is building up and where something needs attention. Colour adds another signal: green for on track, amber for slipping, red for needing attention, grey for not started. Even after a role is filled, its clock tells a story: you can see where the process moved well and where it took longer than expected, rather than just the final time-to-fill. The visual should make the parts that a single total hides visible at a glance, then help us ask better questions.

### A nod to Florence Nightingale

I'd be wrong not to mention Florence Nightingale. Her famous 1858 polar area diagram, now often called a coxcomb chart, made the causes of soldiers' deaths during the Crimean War visible at a glance. The visual resemblance is there, and it deserves an acknowledgement.

This has been seven months in the making. I'm interested to hear whether we'll see this appear in TA reporting and analytics over the next year.

Darren Bush, Founder, TA Unboxed. The Fluid Clock is a concept I've been developing as part of a broader intelligence framework for TA.

## Newsletter

Sponsor (Metaview, flag as sponsor content): fillmore, "an autonomous sourcing agent. You tell it who you need. It researches, contacts, and schedules the most relevant candidates for the role, 24/7, without you driving each step." "It books you a pipeline of candidates who already want to speak to you."

By Darren Bush. "We ask candidates to prove they’re genuine. How easy do we make it for them to check that we are?" Follows Tuesday's issue on fake recruiters (2026-11-10).

"We spend a lot of time asking whether candidates are genuine. We check their experience, identity, assessments and references. But what are we doing to make sure the recruitment process itself is trustworthy?"

**When the recruiter is the fraudster.** "Impersonators can create believable recruiter profiles, imitate legitimate employers and run convincing interviews. They may be after personal details, payment or access to a device." "The recent Binance case illustrated a more unsettling possibility: an employee exploring another job can be targeted through a malicious technical assessment, creating a potential route into their current organisation." Two responsibilities: protect the candidates in your process, and protect your employees approached by fake recruiters elsewhere.

**Verification shouldn't depend on the message itself.** Stacy Zapar's Might Be Fake resource at Tenfold "brings together documented fraud signals and reports of recruiters being impersonated." Her Fake Stacy account: "impostors can reuse a genuine recruiter’s name, branding and LinkedIn details while changing the actual contact address. That’s why a polished signature or convincing profile isn’t reliable proof." The resource is "an investigation aid, not a definitive blacklist"; names on its Catfish List "identify people being impersonated, not offenders."

Author's proposal: "The official careers site should provide an independently accessible, secure candidate portal and explain approved email domains and recruitment channels." "Email should notify. The verified portal should confirm."

**Five design principles** (built from Luke Eaton's practical recommendations):

1. Make your recruiters verifiable. "Publish official contact channels and explain which domains and interview platforms you use. Make it easy to verify a vacancy through your careers site rather than a link from an unknown person."
2. Tell candidates what you will never ask. A candidate safety page could say what you won't request early on, whether you ever charge fees, and how you handle assessments and software: "candidates should be cautious about requests to install unfamiliar software, run code or provide banking information outside an appropriate, verified process."
3. Give people a safe reporting route. "Keep a record of patterns, false profiles and domains so TA and Security can act together." "Acknowledge reports and submissions you receive."
4. Protect employees without policing their careers. Citing Matt Buckland on private job searches: "Would you tell your employer you’d clicked a suspicious link during an interview for another job?" "An employer should make clear that staff can seek confidential security advice about suspicious external job approaches without disclosing their career intentions to their manager." Suggests calling out examples in the next 2027 mandatory training.
5. Make checks proportionate and collaborative. "Use appropriate identity checks, clear assessment rules, secure onboarding and targeted escalation when risks justify them." Points to Pinpoint's candidate fraud webinar and framework (vendor content) and Stacy Zapar on the candidate fraud epidemic.

"Simpler: Make real jobs, recruiters and official channels easy to verify. Better: Give candidates and employees clear guidance and safe reporting routes. Different: Design recruitment safety jointly across TA, HR and Cybersecurity". "Trust shouldn’t be an extra step. It should be designed into hiring."

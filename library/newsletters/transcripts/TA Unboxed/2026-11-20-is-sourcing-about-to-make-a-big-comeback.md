What’s the largest number of applications you’ve ever received for a single job?

500? 2,000? 10,000?

The highest I’ve read about is a role receiving 18,000 applications. Despite that volume, the team reportedly sourced candidates directly.

18,000 applications, and they still had to go looking. Yikes.

John Vlastelica describes the challenge well. We used to be looking for a needle in a haystack. Now we’re looking for a needle in a stack of needles.

We all know now that AI makes applying easier. More applications look polished, tailored and, funnily enough, very similar to the Job Ad you posted.

I don’t know about you, but my LinkedIn feed is full of sourcing posts right now. So, today we’re going to look at:

Is sourcing about to make a big comeback?

### Is LinkedIn Recruiter still the centre of sourcing?

For years, LinkedIn Recruiter has dominated sourcing. Most TA teams have built their ways of working around it and invested heavily in licences.

But I’m seeing more people question whether it should still be the default.

I recently saw a team post how a four-person team at a venture firm had helped make more than 250 hires in three and a half years, mainly through networks and outbound sourcing.

They’ve reduced their LinkedIn Recruiter spend.

That’s not a blueprint for every enterprise recruiting team. But it is a reason to ask whether our sourcing spend is still going to the right places.

I’m not suggesting we all abandon LinkedIn Recruiter. I am suggesting we experiment. What if we set aside 20% of our sourcing technology budget or capacity to test alternatives? Not a universal benchmark, just a practical challenge.

Compare the candidates discovered, shortlist quality, response rates, recruiter hours and eventual hiring outcomes. Keep whatever earns its place.

### What if sourcing started before the job was advertised?

Most of us still start with a vacancy: agree the brief, post the advert, process the applications and source if the shortlist is weak.

What if we brought sourcing forward for roles you would typically advertise?

- Map the market. Understand where the talent exists, which organisations employ them and what skills are realistically available.
- Rediscover candidates. Search our existing ATS and CRM for people we’ve already met, assessed or engaged with.
- Explore networks. Ask hiring managers, employees and even candidates who else we should speak to.
- Outbound sourcing. Tools such as Metaview’s fillmore can help with candidate discovery and outreach, making it easier to test interest in the market and start conversations earlier. (Mentions the issue's sponsor: flag as sponsor content.)
- Build relationships early. Start conversations before an urgent vacancy arises.

Imagine arriving at a hiring kickoff knowing which companies have the skills, which candidates we’ve previously engaged, what the market is telling us and who we might approach.

That intelligence could shape the brief before we spend money advertising it.

### The time we get back matters

For me, that’s the opportunity. It’s not about sending more messages for its own sake. It’s about giving sourcers and recruiters more time to understand people, build relationships and advise the business.

There’s an old sourcing technique worth bringing back alongside the new tools.

Glen Cathey calls it meta-sourcing: sourcing people through the people you’ve already sourced.

When you speak to someone who isn’t interested or available, don’t automatically end the conversation. Once you’ve explained the opportunity, ask whether they’ve worked with anyone they rate highly who might be worth speaking to.

People in the market often know brilliant people we’d never find through a keyword search. You can even track introductions generated from sourcing conversations as a team measure, alongside replies and hires, without rewarding people for pushing unwanted referrals.

That’s another reason I think sourcing is becoming more valuable. Every conversation can produce a candidate, a connection or intelligence for the next search.

My advice? Start experimenting.

Give your team permission, time and budget to explore what else is possible.

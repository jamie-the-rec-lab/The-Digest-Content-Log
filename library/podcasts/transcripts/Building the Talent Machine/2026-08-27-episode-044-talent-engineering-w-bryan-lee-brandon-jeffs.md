## Actionable summary
**Guest:** Bryan Lee, sourcer and co-founder of Subgraph, a bootstrapped two person search firm, with prior experience at Sequoia Capital, Uber, Apple and Google, and founder of the earlier search firm Narwhal Search.  ·  **Show:** Building the Talent Machine, 27 Aug 2026
**Tags:** building ai tools, ai sourcing, rec tech, sourcing, talent mapping, recruiter workflow, startup hiring, staffing and agencies

**When to use this:** Your team is weighing whether to build internal AI recruiting tools versus buy them, or a leader is celebrating vibe coded automation without weighing the compliance and outcome risk.

**Big idea:** AI tooling should target the specific repetitive step that eats a recruiter's time, not an attempt to automate the whole workflow, and every tool or build has to be judged by whether it actually produces more hires, not by how impressive the technology is.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Audit your own calendar and daily workflow for the single most repetitive, time consuming task (Subgraph's engineer noticed candidate submissions were taking 5 to 10 minutes each and built a tool just to speed up that one step) rather than trying to automate sourcing end to end.
- Judge any AI sourcing tool or internal build by whether it increases actual hires, not by pipeline size; Lee deliberately ran Subgraph on a contingent, pay-on-hire basis at launch so the business only made money if the tooling produced hires.
- Before green lighting an internal "talent engineer" build, ask what happens when it breaks: who spends the hours troubleshooting, and does the underlying workflow depend on someone with deep systems and security background (Subgraph's build is led by a CTO, not a recruiter learning to code).
- When a market is saturated for a narrow requirement, quantify it explicitly to the hiring manager, for example telling them there are only around 800 people in a given state with the exact skill set, then negotiate whether to widen geography, relax the profile, or wait.
- Treat automating outreach activity on LinkedIn as a real compliance risk, not a technical question: sending InMails or connection requests via automation breaches LinkedIn's terms, and accounts have been suspended for it, so weigh whether the speed gain is worth risking a personal LinkedIn brand built over years.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- Subgraph, a two person team, closed six placements in one recent month, its most successful stretch to date, attributed to being more selective about which markets to pursue rather than only chasing San Francisco.
- Around 20% of Subgraph's hires last year came from sourcing originally done for a different role, which is prompting them to build ways to resurface strong candidates against other open roles.
- Lee flags that current AI sourcing tool pricing (he cites around $200 a month with cancel anytime) is subsidised by frontier AI labs' API costs, similar to early subsidised Uber rides, so true unit economics of these tools are not yet visible.

**Contrarian take:**
- Lee argues that going after a company's "top 20 CS school, five years, pre series C" candidate profile just borrows someone else's hiring signal rather than defining what actually predicts success, and cites hiring a strong candidate from Cisco after 14 years of assuming that background would not fit smaller companies.
- He is sceptical of teams that claim to be "solving recruiting" broadly, arguing that if the problem were solvable with pure technology it already would have been, and that narrower, workflow specific tools (his own included) are the more honest bet.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Pick the one recurring task that eats the most of your week (submissions, scheduling, note taking) and look for a narrow tool or workflow fix for that step specifically, instead of chasing an all in one AI sourcing platform.
- Before automating any LinkedIn outreach, check whether it breaches LinkedIn's terms of service and weigh that against the personal brand and network risk if your account gets flagged.
- When a hiring manager's requirement is too narrow, quantify the size of the addressable talent pool for them so the negotiation about widening criteria has a real number behind it.

Bryan Lee is a sourcer, recruiting operator, and co-founder of Subgraph with experience at Sequoia Capital, Uber, Apple, and Google. In this episode of Building the Talent Machine, Bryan joins host Brandon Jeffs to explore the future of talent sourcing, AI recruiting, talent engineering, recruiting automation, and startup hiring.

As artificial intelligence changes recruiting, sourcing is becoming about more than finding candidates. Recruiters now have access to AI sourcing tools, talent intelligence platforms, automation, LLMs, and increasingly sophisticated ways to map talent markets. The question is whether those tools actually make recruiters more effective and lead to more hires.

Bryan explains how Subgraph approaches recruiting technology by building around the real workflow of a search firm. Rather than trying to automate the entire recruiting process, the team looks for repetitive work that technology can make faster while preserving recruiter judgment, candidate relationships, and hiring outcomes.

Brandon and Bryan also discuss the emerging talent engineer role and why recruiters are beginning to build their own internal recruiting tools and AI workflows.

## Actionable summary
**Guest:** Colby Kennedy Nesbitt, organisational psychologist and Senior Manager of Employee Listening at Netflix, with a background in industrial organisational psychology, writing a Substack called Variance Explained.  ·  **Show:** Building the Talent Machine, 28 Aug 2026
**Tags:** recruiting metrics, quality of hire, ta leadership, talent market data, retention, employer branding, human-ai collaboration, ai strategy

**When to use this:** You are being asked by leadership to defend or define "quality of hire" or "top 1% talent" and need a sharper way to answer than the usual dashboard metrics.

**Big idea:** Headcount, tenure, quality of hire and performance ratings are all proxies that fail to measure what businesses actually want, which is whether a person is creating value, and the topline metric founders are now flexing about is revenue per employee rather than headcount growth.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Stop treating quality of hire as a defined metric. Nesbitt argues it lacks a universal definition across companies, so when a hiring manager arrives with their own memory of what it meant at their last company, using the same term without shared meaning just injects friction and makes HR look inconsistent.
- Use the IO psychology distinction between typical performance (what someone does day to day, when nobody is watching, driven by motivation) and maximum performance (what someone does in an interview or under observation, driven by ability) to explain why a 30 minute interview mostly measures the wrong one.
- To get closer to typical performance signal, use references, a paid work sample, or a short probationary or contract to hire period rather than relying on interview performance alone, while recognising the most in demand candidates will often refuse this.
- Reframe "we hire the top 1%" claims for stakeholders: there is no counterfactual data on the candidates not hired, so the claim cannot be disproven or proven, and it is usually really a signal that the company is trying to say it hires the 99th percentile of the market for that specific role, not a measured fact.
- When copying another company's culture practices (ping pong tables, radical candour, a famous culture memo), check for internal consistency with your own business and values first. Borrowed trappings without that fit produce what Nesbitt calls organ rejection.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- US median employee tenure has fallen from about four and a half years to just under four years per Bureau of Labor Statistics data Nesbitt cites, and tenure in tech is often estimated closer to two years.
- Brandon notes frontier AI labs like Anthropic are hiring senior B2B SaaS talent at 75th to 90th percentile compensation, pulling experienced people out of the wider software talent pool.

**Contrarian take:**
- Nesbitt argues headcount growth was never a reliable proxy for productivity (one strong performer can be worth ten times an average one), and that link has now visibly broken as companies cut headcount while revenue rises.
- She also argues shortened tenure means some leaders make a few "splashy" decisions and move on before being around to see the consequences, so a fast track record can look like a maximum performance illusion rather than typical, sustained motivation.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Before agreeing to a quality of hire metric with a hiring manager, ask them to define exactly what it means to them first, since the term almost never carries the same definition across companies.
- When a stakeholder asks you to hire "the top 1%," reframe the conversation around what specific compensation percentile and experience trade-offs you are actually targeting for that role.
- If copying a well known company's culture practice, check it is internally consistent with your own business model and values rather than adopting it as a surface signal.

## Episode description

Colby Kennedy Nesbitt is an organizational psychologist, talent strategist, people analytics leader, and Senior Manager of Employee Listening at Netflix. In this episode of Building the Talent Machine, Colby joins host Brandon Jeffs for a wide-ranging conversation about people analytics, recruiting metrics, employee listening, talent strategy, organizational psychology, AI, workplace culture, and how companies actually measure human performance and business value.

Colby’s work sits at the intersection of data and organizational decision-making. She believes people data should clarify and empower rather than intimidate, and that the most important part of an analysis is often how effectively its meaning is communicated to business leaders.

The conversation starts with a fundamental question: are HR and recruiting teams measuring the right things?

Brandon and Colby challenge traditional recruiting and workforce metrics including quality of hire, headcount growth, tenure, performance ratings, and the idea that companies can objectively claim to hire the “top 1%” of talent.

They explore the shift from headcount growth as a proxy for company success toward metrics like revenue per employee and actual business value. They also discuss why hiring more people does not automatically create a more productive organization and why the value created by an employee can look completely different across sales, engineering, operations, manufacturing, and executive roles.

## Actionable summary
**Guest:** Mike Clapson, VP of Sales at Ashby, an early employee (almost five years, joined when Ashby was under 30 people) who runs sales hiring and has previously worked at SAP, Mobify and Slack. He is an internal guest and Ashby is an ATS vendor that also sponsors the show, so this is a vendor describing its own process.  ·  **Show:** Offer Accepted (host Shannon Ogborn), 6 Oct 2026
**Tags:** interview design, structured interviewing, scorecards, candidate calibration, interviewer training, gtm hiring, candidate experience, hiring manager alignment, retention, internal mobility

**Vendor flags:** Ashby sells the recruiting platform and the episode opens and closes with its advert. Results such as attrition and referrals are self reported, relayed second hand, and not linked to the interview changes by any analysis. Treat them as anecdote.

**When to use this:** a high volume sales hiring process has too many rounds, candidates are dropping out, or the team is adding interviews to "derisk" hires.

**Big idea:** Audit every round for the unique signal it adds, remove any round whose score or evidence you can already get earlier, and run the removal as an experiment.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Cut rounds by looking at the scores. Ashby's cross functional culture fit interview sat in the final round and almost always produced a score of 3, so they inferred that signal earlier and removed it without feeling they lost anything. The process went from five rounds to four, then three, over roughly two years, and the current process has run for a year to a year and a half.
- Ask of each round whether there is earlier evidence of the same attribute. Clapson's test is whether a stand alone interview is necessary or whether the signal can be picked up in a prior round.
- Take the CEO out of high volume account executive hiring once calibration is strong. He still joins leadership and solutions engineering hires.
- The three round AE loop is a recruiter screen, then an hour with the hiring manager (about 45 minutes on the candidate's experience including a deal win review), then a "challenge round". For mid market and enterprise the challenge round is a demo role play followed by a deal strategy interview.
- Let candidates do the role play twice. They go once, reflect and take feedback, then go again, which tests openness to feedback.
- Cap preparation and level the field. Candidates are told the demo should take no more than about two hours, and get the same recorded videos and help centre docs on two named topics. Referred candidates are told the interviewer can say what the company does but must not coach them on how to pass.
- Use scenario questions ("what would you do if") instead of "tell me about a time". Candidates may ask for more information, and you watch how they piece together a logical answer, which suits people whose current role is not structured like yours.
- Calibrate new interviewers with shadows. Clapson says Ashby learned from its engineering team that each interviewer does three shadows then three reverse shadows, with a short debrief after each. Ashby went from three or four hiring managers to about 14 in the period, and calibrated on what a 3 versus a 4 means.
- Do live debriefs for every candidate who reaches the final round, and watch for the themes a senior leader keeps probing.
- Before changing an inherited process, understand why it exists. Clapson cites the Chesterton's fence parable (he says "Chesterman's") as advice for new managers.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- Clapson says moving a strong candidate from application to offer in 21 days instead of 30 or 40 "is going to give you an advantage". This is an illustration, not a measured result.
- Attrition on the sales and revops team (about 90 people) is down about 20% year over year, relayed from the head of people.
- Roughly 40% or more of Ashby's business comes from referrals and community, which he links to hiring people customers want to work with.
- Clapson claims "at least a 10x ROI" on intake clarity and cites 30 to 60 minutes up front with three or four people against dozens of wasted interviewing hours. This is his opinion, not a study.

**Contrarian take:**
- Clapson notes that other companies are adding rounds to derisk hires, while Ashby cut rounds and kept a high bar. He says three rounds is the minimum and they cannot go shorter.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Pull the last 20 scorecards from your final round and check whether one interview scores nearly everyone the same. If so, trial removing it on the next role.
- Tell candidates the full list of rounds in the first call, and state how long the take home or prep should take.

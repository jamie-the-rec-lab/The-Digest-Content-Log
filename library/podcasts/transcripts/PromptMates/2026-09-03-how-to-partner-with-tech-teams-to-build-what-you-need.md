**Guest:** Nickhil Tekwani, Senior Manager Applied AI at OpenGov, who leads the internal team that builds AI tools for non-technical staff across a company serving over 2,000 US state and local governments  ·  **Host:** Jason Miller, PromptMates  ·  **Show:** PromptMates, 3 Sep 2026

**When to use this:** you or your team keep asking a technical partner for "a chatbot" and the build either stalls or misses the actual problem.

**Big idea:** most requests for AI help are really requests to fix broken data plumbing, so the job before any build is describing the problem, not naming a solution.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Sort every incoming request into one of three buckets before building anything: something the requester can do themselves, a light build needing collaboration between a technical and non-technical person, or a real engineering task.
- Before agreeing to build anything, ask what the requester actually wants to happen, because a request for "a chatbot" often turns out to really mean getting data onto a BI dashboard.
- A real example from the episode: a team spent one person two days a week checking 150 to 200 staff on leave across 170 separate spreadsheets; the fix was consolidating all 170 into one Google Sheet driven by a Google Apps Script, built in about an hour using Claude to write the script, with no AI in the final solution.
- As a non-technical person's fluency grows, expect your requests to move up the value chain: from writing a prompt, to writing a skill, to building an MCP, to needing full engineering support, so keep raising the difficulty of what you attempt.
- When acting as the technical partner (a "forward deployed engineer"), open with four questions: what do you do and how does your area work, what's driving you crazy, where are you in your AI journey, and which two systems do you want talking to each other.
- Run that four-question conversation with five to seven people in the same function to surface a shared theme, for example many people separately wanting two systems to sync.

**Contrarian take:**
- Nickhil argues technical people most often go wrong by reaching for the cleanest or most technically interesting solution rather than the one that actually solves the user's problem, a risk he says AI has made worse because building now happens so fast.
- Jason argues the deepest barrier for non-technical staff isn't imposter syndrome about AI generally, it's a specific fear of talking to developers, and treats imposter syndrome as a signal you're moving in the right direction rather than something to avoid.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Before your next AI request to a technical team, write down the actual problem in one sentence before you write down the tool you think you need.
- Ask a technical collaborator your four kickoff questions on your next project: what do you do, what's driving you crazy, where are you with AI, and what two systems should talk to each other.

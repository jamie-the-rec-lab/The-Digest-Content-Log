## Actionable summary
**Guest:** James Ellis, founder of Employer Brand Labs and author of five books on employer branding, with 13 years across agency, consulting and in-house employer brand work.  ·  **Show:** Recruiting Future, 28 Aug 2026
**Tags:** employer branding, employer storytelling, evp, recruitment marketing, ai market research, candidate experience, rec tech, ta leadership

**When to use this:** Your employer brand or careers content sounds identical to every competitor's and you need a way to find and prove a genuine point of difference without a large agency budget.

**Big idea:** Employer branding has shifted from a competition to look attractive (a strategy that worked when most career sites were badly built) to a competition to prove genuine difference with evidence, because now that every career site looks polished, looking good no longer creates any advantage.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Scrape your own and your competitors' career sites, LinkedIn posts and Glassdoor content (Ellis used AI tools to scrape every Fortune 500 career site plus each company's last 40 LinkedIn posts into one spreadsheet) and ask an AI tool to summarise what each company claims to offer in ten words or less, so you can see where your own claims overlap with everyone else's.
- Treat universally claimed values (caring about people, innovation, mission) as table stakes rather than differentiators, the way "cares about patient outcomes" is table stakes for a biotech, and instead identify the specific thing your competitors are not talking about.
- Turn a vague claim into a provable one by attaching a real number pulled from internal records. Ellis's example: a company that said "we care about our people" turned that into "we spent $147,000 last year sending people chicken soup when they were sick," verified by the CFO's tallied credit card bills.
- Mine internal sources for proof before hiring an agency: engagement survey data, onboarding materials, culture decks, and even how people actually talk to each other in Slack or Teams channels, since real employee behaviour is harder to fake and cherry pick than corporate copy.
- Once you have found a genuine point of difference, aim to "own" that specific space in your positioning so directly that a candidate reading your careers content thinks "I didn't even know I could want that in a job," rather than restating a claim every competitor already makes.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- In Ellis's Fortune 500 scrape, he found ten companies with functionally identical taglines despite having nothing to do with each other, including IBM matched against a dialysis provider.
- The proof example he cites: a company quantified its "we care about our people" claim as $147,000 spent on Door Dash meal deliveries to sick employees over 12 months.

**Contrarian take:**
- Ellis argues that playing it safe with bland, universally acceptable language is now the riskiest strategy available, because when everyone sounds pleasant and inoffensive, no message stands out enough to move a candidate.
- He also argues smaller companies benchmarking themselves against Google or another famous brand ("what does Google do, let's do that but smaller") guarantees losing, since copying a bigger, better resourced competitor's positioning at a smaller scale produces only a weaker version of something that already exists.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Pull your last few months of careers page copy, job ads and LinkedIn posts alongside three competitors' equivalents and check for overlapping claims and language.
- Take one vague values claim your company makes and find or ask for a real internal number that proves it (spend, participation rate, policy detail) rather than leaving it as an unverifiable adjective.
- Ask a few employees informally how they would describe a moment the company backed up a stated value, and treat any concrete story that surfaces as raw material for your employer brand messaging.

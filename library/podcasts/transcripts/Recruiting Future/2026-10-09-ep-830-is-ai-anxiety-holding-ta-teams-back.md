**Guest:** Jennifer Rozon, Division President at McLean & Company, a global HR research and advisory firm (the transcript renders her surname as "Rosen"; the episode description spells it Rozon)  ·  **Show:** Recruiting Future, Ep830, 9 Oct 2026 (sponsored by Vonq)
**Tags:** ai upskilling, ta leadership, human-ai collaboration, early career talent, recruiter training, ai strategy, recruiting enablement, quality of hire

**When to use this:** your recruiting team is hesitant to use AI tools, or leadership is talking about cutting junior roles because of AI.

**Big idea:** AI adoption stalls when people feel threatened, so leaders must acknowledge the fear, give permission to experiment and be clear about which roles are affected, and must fix the surrounding systems, not only the tools and skills.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Use 4 leadership behaviours to move people from anxiety to confidence: acknowledge the fear, reduce uncertainty, build confidence and turn fear into dialogue.
- Give 3 things explicitly: permission to try, visibility into what others learn from using AI, and clarity on what will and will not change roles.
- Review the five "leadership ecosystem" enablers from McLean's research: organisational design, performance drivers, leadership pipelines, leadership development and culture. Her test questions include whether structures match the speed AI needs, whether you reward the behaviours you say you want, and whether culture quietly tells people to play it safe.
- Audit your programmes (competency frameworks, performance management, succession planning, reward and recognition) for whether they reinforce or inhibit the behaviours AI transformation needs, and partner with the head of IT rather than going alone.
- Redesign entry-level roles around the work AI creates, such as making judgement calls, rather than preserving or deleting them.
- Make leadership development continuous and on demand (microlearning, coaching, feedback in performance conversations) alongside structured learning.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- McLean trends research: only 12% of respondents reported not experiencing change fatigue.
- Cognitive debt, as she defined it: a gradual decline in judgement, reasoning and critical thinking from offloading too much thinking to AI, a particular risk for early career staff. She also noted AI's tendency to agree with users.

**Contrarian take:**
- Her son's calculator analogy: teachers were right but solving for the wrong risk. The danger is not understanding the maths well enough to know what to ask and whether the answer makes sense.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- In your next team meeting, state plainly which roles AI will and will not change in the next year, and set aside a small amount of time for people to share what they tried.
- Ask whether your performance review rewards trying AI or only hitting last year's targets.

## Episode description

There is a lot of pressure on talent acquisition to adopt AI, and the discussion about how to do it tends to focus on tools and skills. With layoffs attributed to AI in the headlines, the people in TA teams have good reason to feel anxious about the technology they are being asked to use. That anxiety makes people less willing to experiment, and addressing it has less to do with the technology than with how leaders handle uncertainty. 



So what does it take to move a recruiting team from anxiety about AI to confidence in using it? 



My guest this week is Jennifer Rozon (https://www.linkedin.com/in/jennifer-rozon-3317243/) , Division President of McLean & Company (https://hr.mcleanco.com/) , a global HR research and advisory firm whose recent research looks at leadership and AI transformation. 



In our conversation, Jennifer explains what is driving AI anxiety, how leaders can give their teams the confidence to experiment, and the risks of relying too heavily on AI and cutting entry-level roles.



In the interview, we discuss:


     
       
  •  What is driving AI anxiety in the workforce?
     
     

     
       
  •  Why AI anxiety should not be mistaken for resistance to technology
     
     

     
       
  •  Leading through uncertainty when you don't have all the answers
     
     

     
       
  •  How leaders can give their teams the confidence to experiment
     
     

     
       
  •  How structures, incentives and culture can work against leaders

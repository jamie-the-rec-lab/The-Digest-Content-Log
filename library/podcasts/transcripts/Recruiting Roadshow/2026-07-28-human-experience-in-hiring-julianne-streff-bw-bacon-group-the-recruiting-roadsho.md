## Actionable summary

**Guest:** Julianne Streff, Principal Recruiter at BW Bacon Group; scaled Kin + Carta from startup to IPO as first recruiter  ·  **Show:** Recruiting Roadshow, 28 Jul 2026
**Tags:** candidate experience, recruiter career growth, hiring manager alignment, employer branding, hiring manager training, recruiter productivity, recruiting operations, recruiter workflow

**When to use this:** your hiring process is fast but your offer acceptance rate is low, or your hiring managers feel unprepared going into interviews.

**Big idea:** The core of scaling recruiting is not process or automation; it is empathy and preparation. A stretched-thin recruiting team that automates screening but has no capacity to treat candidates like people will fail on employer brand and quality of hire. Hiring managers with 30 minutes of prep time outperform those without it.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Listen between the words. When a hiring manager briefs you on a role, note what they do not say as much as what they do. Ask clarifying questions. When a candidate opens up about neurodiversity or a career gap, that is signal, connect them to a hiring manager with the same profile or a manager who cares deeply about that trait.
- The recruiter's job is to create psychological safety. Make candidates comfortable through body language, eye contact, affirmation, and genuine empathy. Do not repeat information they have already shared in prior interviews; hiring managers should spend 30 minutes reading the resume and notes beforehand, not asking "tell me about yourself" for the fourth time.
- Deliver rejection feedback as a human, not an AI system. A rejection email after three rounds of interviews from an automation is a brand killer. If someone has invested time, they deserve to hear "you are seen, heard, and valued" from a person.
- Own the decision-making clarity. Early in a startup, the recruiter and CEO decide together. At scale, clarify: is the recruiter a veto or advisory? Do hiring managers have the final say or is hiring a joint decision? Document this and review it quarterly as you grow.
- Hire for potential, not Goldilocks. The market is too unpredictable to hire for the perfect fit. Screen for: connection to company values, baseline skills to contribute in the first 90 days, and demonstrated willingness to learn. A former investment banker or web designer in a non-technical role can ship.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- Pass-through rate to interviews (how many candidates screened yields one interview) signals whether you understand what you are hiring for. High numbers indicate confusion or market mismatch.
- Offer acceptance rate is telling. If it is low, you misunderstood either the candidate or the hiring manager, or both.
- Time to fill and SLA discipline: understand whether a hiring manager is urgent or has flexibility. A manager who says "I have all the time in the world" will have a different process pace than one asking for 21 days.

**Tools & prompts:**
- No specific tools or prompts were named in the interview.

**Contrarian take:**
- Julianne: most organisations automate screening and think they have solved hiring. They miss the human side entirely. Automation is only good if it frees a recruiter to do the work humans do best: listen, connect, build trust, and create safety.
- Reject automated rejection letters. Yes, it saves time. No, it does not scale if you care about employer brand. A candidate who invested time deserves to hear it from a person.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Brief your hiring managers: spend 30 minutes before each interview reading the resume, prior interview notes, and the scorecard for the role. Do not ask questions already answered in writing. Track compliance for two weeks.
- Listen for the unspoken. In your next hiring manager kick-off, take notes on what they do not say about the role. Follow up: "What are you worried about this hire?" or "What would be a dealbreaker?"
- If you have an offer acceptance rate below 75%, run an exit survey: did the candidate feel they understood the role? Did they feel the hiring manager was prepared? Were they treated with respect throughout? Fix one thing.

## Actionable summary
**Guest:** Stefanie Fackrell, Founding Recruiting Lead at Glimpse (an AI startup), previously full-cycle recruiter at Google, Apple's camera team and Nvidia  ·  **Show:** The Recruiting Roadshow, 16 Sep 2026
**Tags:** ai screening, sourcing, recruiter career growth, candidate fraud, structured interviewing, human-ai collaboration, early-stage hiring

**When to use this:** you are a small or early-stage team's sole recruiter trying to protect your time and the hiring manager's time without losing signal quality, or you are deciding how much weight to give a resume's formatting.

**Big idea:** the same forces that make sourcing hard right now, thousands of AI-assisted applications that all look the same, are also the reason AI screening at the top of the funnel is becoming necessary rather than optional, as long as a human stays in charge of what "good" looks like.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- On resume screening, Fackrell trusts data over formatting: "as long as the data is good" a rough format is fine, but she flags AI-written resumes by their identical structure and identical-sounding summary paragraphs, and looks for concrete achievement bullet points rather than "word salad or keyword stuffing."
- She uses AI for the initial phone screen selectively at volume, reasoning that if 200 candidates apply and only 50 would get a human review anyway, an AI first pass functions like one-way video interviewing did originally: it lets more candidates get a shot rather than fewer.
- At Glimpse she trains the AI screen against the hiring manager's actual intake criteria, then only passes the "top 10, 15% of people through" to "really value the time of those senior leaders." Note the current state versus her stated ideal: at Glimpse "the CEO the CTO and COO are still speaking to every single candidate. until we build in that middle management", and only then does she think "it should only be the finalists, like the two, three finalists." The finalists-only model is what she argues for, not what is happening now. (Corrected 2026-09-17: this line previously read "who still speak to every finalist at this stage", which misread the transcript at 12:59.)
- She makes a go/no-go call after her own initial screen rather than always checking with the hiring manager first: "I know in my initial screen if someone's going to make it through or not," so she skips the check-in step for candidates who are clearly not going to move forward, saving both her time and the hiring manager's.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- "This client Glimpse I have I've been there for nine weeks I've hired 18 people so I've grown the company by 65%," offered as her own headline signal that recruiting is working
- Running roughly "20 to 30 interviews a week" solo across 15 open roles during that nine-week stretch

**Contrarian take:** Fackrell pushes back on the idea that a polished, keyword-optimized resume is what recruiters should reward, arguing that "we're over complicating what a resume should be" and that good bullet points beat good formatting every time.

**Steal this (do tomorrow):**
- Before opening a requisition, run an intake conversation that names concrete quality signals rather than pedigree markers like top universities or brand-name employers, and use those signals to train whatever AI screening you run.
- When your own initial screen tells you a candidate is a clear no, skip the hiring-manager check-in call for that candidate rather than running everyone through the same confirmation step.

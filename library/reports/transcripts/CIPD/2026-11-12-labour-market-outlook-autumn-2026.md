### Foreword
Confidence is at the heart of everything right now. Businesses and households have been
sitting on cash and paying down debt in the face of uncertainty and rising inflation. This
is individually sensible, but collectively challenging. Without cash flowing to investment,
growth, job creation and sustainable pay rises are all damaged.
The quarterly CIPD Labour Market Outlook (LMO) uses a representative survey of more
than 2,000 employers to give us insight into the decisions employers are making every day.
Rising confidence is something we would see quickly in this survey. But this quarter’s report
remains subdued with relatively few employers planning to increase their headcount and
hiring intentions depressed.
Given rising costs this autumn, this outlook is not a surprise. Inflation is climbing again,
energy costs increasing and significant new regulatory costs are arriving as further
Employment Rights Act measures come into force.
Looking ahead, the picture of a challenging external and economic environment is unlikely
to change given the Autumn Budget failed to provide any significant measures to reduce the
cost of doing business and employing people. The decision to pause equalising the youth
National Minimum Wage rate with the adult rate is welcome and may help stem the loss of
jobs for young people but does nothing to reverse the very significant rise in employment
cost organisations have faced over the last two years, particularly aimed at entry-level
workers through changes to the threshold for employer National Insurance payment.
Rather than making things better, the current future policy framework still looks as though
it will make things worse. If there are not sensible compromises made on proposed
reforms to the use of zero hours contracts there could be a further significant move away
from job creation at the margins, having the most damaging impact on young people and
those returning to the labour market. As well as being a tragedy for the individual workers
involved, this will slow progress on reducing the benefits bill and on growing the economy
– a double blow in terms of resolving the fiscal challenges the UK faces.
It will be important that the Milburn plan to tackle youth unemployment sets out a joined-up
approach to boosting training and job opportunities for young people that recognises the need
to ensure our employment rights framework supports rather than undermines job creation.
All of this is set in the context of a labour market changing faster than ever as AI
increasingly reshapes workplace practices, jobs and the labour market. This quarter’s
report highlights how widespread the use of AI is across organisations and the potential
for effective adoption to improve job performance, particularly where it has been
integrated across business functions and operations.
While the majority of organisations are not currently expecting to reduce headcount
because of AI in the next 12 months, those employers who are further ahead on their
adoption journey are more likely to report future changes to jobs. These employers are
more likely to expect both job loss and job creation due to having a
better understanding of how to adapt work and skills alongside its use.
Optimising the benefits of AI will require a stable and supportive policy
and business environment which can underpin skills investment and
maximise employment growth as the technology changes tasks and jobs,
and aid organisations in using AI safely and productively.
Read on for our latest labour market data and analysis on employers’
recruitment, redundancy and pay intentions this autumn.
Neil Carberry
Chief Executive, CIPD

### Key points
• The net employment balance – the difference between employers expecting there will be an increase in staffing levels and those expecting there will be a decrease in the next three months – is +10, remaining subdued for the seventh consecutive quarter. (Chart: +21 autumn 2024, +9 autumn 2025, +10 autumn 2026.)
• Net employment intentions in the public sector have increased but remain near zero (+1).
• Only 58% of employers in the private sector plan to recruit over the next three months – this figure is broadly unchanged for the last seven quarters.
• The median expected basic pay increase remains at 3% overall, and across all sectors, with real pay likely to fall in the coming months.
• More than four in five (83%) employers have adopted AI, with the majority, two in five (40%), at the ad hoc/experimental stage.
• Private sector SMEs lag behind in AI adoption, with 29% reporting no AI use. Deep integration is most common among large private sector employers (15%).
• One in five (21%) employers believe AI will reduce their headcount over the next 12 months, rising to one in four (25%) among employers that know AI is used in their organisation. The majority (61%) believe AI adoption will make no difference to their headcount over the next 12 months.
• 62% of employers using AI report better job performance, though mostly by a little (52%) rather than a lot (10%). Among those where AI is deeply integrated, 35% say performance has improved a lot.

### Recruitment and redundancy outlook
The net employment balance (employment intentions) – the difference between
employers expecting there will be an increase in staff levels and those expecting
there will be a decrease in staff levels in the next three months – is still subdued this
quarter at +10 (Figure 1).
The net employment balance declined from its peak in 2021, which marked the
end of the furlough scheme and the start of the war in Ukraine, until 2024, when it
appeared to stabilise to around the 2014–19 average (of +19). However, a sharp fall at
the end of 2024 and the beginning of 2025, in part due to the 2024 Autumn Budget,
led to it falling to the lowest level in the series outside of the pandemic. This spans
the announcement of the rise in the rate of employer National Insurance, and the
reduction in the secondary threshold. Since these policies came into force in April
2025, intentions have remained at record lows. The current employer caution reflects
mounting cost pressures and continued economic uncertainty.

### Job vacancies
Official data shows that in the latest period (July to September 2026) there were
estimated to be 691,000 vacancies in the UK, down from 1.29m at the peak in April
to June 2022. Discounting the pandemic, this is the lowest level of vacancies in the
UK since the same period in 2014. But since then, the labour market has grown. In
2014, there were 30.9 million people in employment. Today that figure stands at
around 34.6 million, an increase of roughly 12%. Fewer openings in a bigger labour
market means it’s significantly harder for jobseekers to find work than the headline
vacancy figure implies.
Our data suggests that despite the low level of vacancies in the economy, employers
continue to struggle with hard-to-fill vacancies. Three in 10 (30%) employers
currently report having them (see Figure 6). This level is unchanged overall, and
in every industry across the past year. This indicates skills mismatches across the
economy, as employers seek talent that is not available to them.

### Pay outlook
Among employers looking to increase, decrease or freeze pay in the next 12 months,
the median expected basic pay increase for the next 12 months has now remained
at 3% overall for 10 consecutive quarters (Figure 9). This means we continue to see
elevated pay growth compared with pre-pandemic levels. They have remained at
3% in the private sector since summer 2024 and 3% in the public sector since spring
2025. With the exception of the summer 2025 wave, the median has also been at 3%
in the third/voluntary sector since summer 2024.
Expected pay awards held at a historic high of 5% between winter 2022/23 and
autumn 2023, before falling to 4% in winter 2023/24 and spring 2024.
Thirty-four per cent of employers are expecting to offer pay rises in the next year
of exactly 3%.
To put the median expected pay award into context, the Consumer Prices Index (CPI)
– the most referred to measure of inflation – in September 2026 rose to 3.5%. While
real pay, over the last three months, remains above zero at 0.8%, the Office for Budget
Responsibility (OBR) is forecasting no real earnings growth in 2027 and 2028.1 This
means many across the economy will receive below-inflation pay rises in the coming
period. Inflation is currently expected to peak in the first half of next year at around
5%, with real disposable household income set to fall.

### AI and UK employers
This quarter we explore the level of AI adoption within UK workplaces, what the
near-term employment impact is expected to be and whether employers report that
AI is leading to better job performance.
More than four in five (83%) employers have adopted AI, with the majority, two in five
(40%), at the ad hoc stage, experimenting with off-the-shelf AI tools (eg AI assistants
like ChatGPT, data analysis tools like PowerBI) (Figure 10), but with no formal strategy
or dedicated resources/integration. In slightly fewer organisations (35%), AI is being
integrated into specific business functions, strategy and operational workflows.
Among 8% of UK employers, AI is deeply integrated into operations and strategy.
Fourteen per cent of employers currently report no AI use that they are aware of.
Use is almost universal in legal, accounting and consultancy (95%), finance and
insurance (95%), public administration (94%) and compulsory education (93%). It is
lowest in hospitality (69%), retail (69%) and construction (75%). Around three in 10
employers in hospitality (31%) and retail (29%) report no AI use at all, double the
average of all employers.
For many employers, AI use is still ad hoc and experimental, with no formal strategy
or integration. This is most common in compulsory education (63%) and the
voluntary sector (59%), and also above average in retail (48%) and healthcare (45%).
Integrating AI into specific business functions is most common in legal, accounting
and consultancy (55%), public administration (52%) and IT (50%). Deep integration
into operations and strategy is higher in finance and insurance (22%) and IT (20%).
Two industries have unusual patterns. Construction is polarised: it has one of the
highest rates of deep integration (19%), but more of its employers than average report
no AI use (22%). Compulsory education shows the reverse: almost all employers use
AI, but most are still at the experimental stage.

The majority of organisations that reported no AI use (84%), and the majority of
those that are at the ad hoc experimental stage (69%), believe AI adoption will make
no difference to their headcount over the next year (Figure 14).
Where it is integrated in some functions, half (52%) believe it will make no difference,
but a third (32%) believe they will reduce headcount in the next year due to AI
adoption. This is starkly different still from employers where it is deeply integrated,
where a third (33%) of employers expect no difference and 44% expect a headcount
reduction. Nearly a fifth (17%) of these employers expect that AI will increase
headcount, more than four times the rate of the average employer.
These findings suggest that AI is increasingly influencing how organisations think
about their future workforce requirements. While most employers do not anticipate
immediate changes to headcount, those that have integrated AI more extensively are
considerably more likely to expect changes in either direction.
On the surface this may appear to support the narrative of significant employment
displacement due to AI, with Microsoft and Barclays recently announcing job losses.
However, it is more likely to reflect findings from our previous research, which
show that those further along in their adoption journey are significantly more likely
be undertaking activity in key areas of workforce transformation. This includes
job redesign, reskilling strategies, and structured approaches to skills intelligence.
Expectations of headcount change among these organisations may therefore reflect
deliberate workforce planning as much as anticipated job cuts, pointing to AI’s
potential to reshape the demand for labour as organisations change how work is
organised and delivered.
Over the longer term, the impact on employment will depend not just on the extent
to which AI enables individual organisations to operate more efficiently, but also on
whether productivity improvements translate into business growth, investment and
new jobs. AI could help address the UK’s longstanding productivity challenges, but
its wider employment benefits will depend on businesses having the confidence
and capacity to grow.

### Recommendations for employers and people practitioners
Plan resourcing with the future pipeline in mind
Reduced recruitment, especially of entry-level and younger workers, risks
compounding future hard-to-fill vacancies once demand recovers. Maintain entry-level and apprenticeship intake even while overall hiring intentions are suppressed.
Get ahead of employment law reform
Keep informed of upcoming Employment Rights Act 2025 changes, particularly the
reforms to zero-hours and low-hours contracts. Understand how the reforms will
impact your business and people processes and implement the necessary changes early
to ensure compliance and mitigate legal claims. Identify where existing HR processes,
systems and management capacity will need to adapt to meet new administrative and
compliance requirements, particularly if operating as an SME or within the public sector,
where resource constraints may compound implementation challenges.
Keep pay decisions fair and transparent
Align reward to organisational performance and communicate decisions clearly.
Balance affordability with fairness to sustain motivation and trust. Consider a financial
wellbeing strategy to help those with money concerns. Support line managers so
that they can have more meaningful conversations with employees about pay and
help manage their expectations, so that their people feel they have been heard,
comprehend the rationale behind the decision, and understand what will happen next.
Take a strategic approach to AI adoption
Move beyond experimentation with individual tools to consider how AI can improve
organisational performance, support innovation and create opportunities for growth.
Avoid focusing solely on short-term efficiency savings and consider how technology
can enable wider changes to business processes and ways of working, so that AI
delivers lasting gains in performance and job quality rather than one-off time savings.
Integrate AI into workforce and skills planning
Assess how AI is changing tasks, skills requirements and future recruitment needs.
Consider the implications for entry-level roles, career development and succession
planning, rather than focusing solely on overall headcount. Without deliberate skills
and work planning, organisations risk weaker capability, more intense work and
disrupted career pathways.
Build capability and track the wider impact of AI
Ensure employees and managers have the skills and support to use AI effectively.
Involve employees in decisions about how AI is introduced and used and develop the
management capabilities needed to redesign jobs and support organisational change.
Monitor productivity and performance gains alongside the effects on job quality,
work intensity and employee development, so that short-term efficiency gains do not
come at the expense of longer-term workforce capability.

## Full report

### Introduction: From tensions to tipping points — Choosing the human advantage

Control or empowerment? Stability or agility? Automation or augmentation? Last year, we explored these tensions and the need to navigate the polarities at play. But in 2026, the pace of change is sharpening the edges of these questions. Organizations are no longer just trying to balance competing forces: They are standing at a tipping point.

In our 2026 Global Human Capital Trends survey, 7 in 10 business leaders say their primary competitive strategy over the next three years is to be fast and nimble—to quickly adapt to and capitalize on changing business, customer or market needs. Leaders also report that the two most important drivers of success are accelerating how people and resources are orchestrated to perform work and increasing their organization's and workforce's ability to adapt to change and speed.

The classic S curve of growth has long described how businesses and work evolve: gradual lift, rapid acceleration, and eventual plateau. Today, that curve is compressing. AI and workforce transformation are accelerating the climb and bringing the plateau sooner. Organizations are pressed to leap to the next curve more quickly to remain competitive. Long cycles of planning and predictable execution may no longer hold in a world where markets, technologies, and worker and customer expectations shift in real time. Success may now depend more on sensing change, experimenting quickly, and adapting continuously. Today, new data and workforce insights—ranging from organizational digital twins to real-time analytics—make it possible to see where an organization sits on the curve and actively steer how and when to jump to the next one.

Historically, organizations jumped the curve by adding new technology, a strategy that may no longer be enough. Organizations will likely need to make the leap differently. Competitive advantage is now primarily less driven by technology differentiation and more by cultivating the human edge. Technology—especially something as increasingly ubiquitous as AI—is replicable. People aren't. Humans create competitive differentiation through adaptivity, creativity, and judgement amid uncertainty and change. When it comes to AI, value is unlocked through a reimagination of work that brings the best of humans and machines together in concert.

Indeed, recent Deloitte research with 100 C-suite leaders reveals that most organizations (59%) are taking a tech-focused approach when it comes to AI. But those taking a tech-focused approach are 1.6x more likely to *not* realize returns on AI investments that exceed expectations compared to those that take a human-centric approach. This human-centric focus allows organizations to confidently jump the curve rather than stay on the same curve, or worse, fall off the curve entirely.

**Three tipping points shaping the future of work.** What makes this moment different is that the pressures on organizations are no longer sequential, but compounding. Technological advancement is converging with economic volatility, geopolitical tensions, societal expectations, and a rapidly shifting workforce. The boundary between planning and execution is collapsing, even as cost pressures, efficiency mandates, and questions of trust and clarity intensify. Many leaders feel overwhelmed—aware of the challenges but struggling to act decisively. Tensions once manageable over time are now tipping points, where hesitation risks missed opportunities and lasting consequences.

In 2026, three tipping points stand out:

- **From human + machine to human x machine.** The boundaries between humans and machines are blurring. Organizations will likely need to redesign work to harness human–machine synergy, moving beyond having humans and machines work side by side. This includes a rethinking of culture, decision rights, and trust in data itself.
- **From cost efficiency to value creation.** Relentless cost pressures, changing consumer and worker behaviors, and geopolitical shifts have pushed many organizations toward efficiency at all costs. But as that model tips, the focus should shift toward value—evolving functions to be fit-for-purpose, investing in innovation, and prioritizing growth through adaptability. Demographic shifts and disappearing workforces are making human capacity itself a scarce resource.
- **From static plans to dynamic orchestration.** The future is both here and unknown, making curiosity a core organizational capability. As strategy and execution merge, organizations will likely need to move beyond structured jobs and workers, orchestrating capacity and capabilities to meet shifting demands. Purpose, values, and culture should evolve from static statements into living parts of the organization.

Each tipping point presents an opportunity for leaders to test new possibilities and accelerate toward the next curve, while also surfacing questions that can no longer be deferred. This year's report focuses on choices that our research shows, despite being powerful levers of value, are often overlooked. The chapters that follow examine these questions in depth: How do we maximize the value of humans and machines working together? How do we know what is true about people and work? Who's accountable when both humans and AI are making decisions? How is AI changing our culture? How do we orchestrate capability and capacity at speed? How do we get more value from our functions? How do we stay relevant?

**Making the leap with human advantage.** Reinvention is no longer episodic: It's the new baseline for work and the workforce. The organizations that thrive will likely be the ones to treat discontinuity as momentum, moving quickly to redesign work, roles, and value rather than reverting to old strategies. As the S-curve compresses, so do the capabilities required to navigate it. Building the human advantage is now as critical as managing technology itself. Those that make bold, intentional choices to strengthen their human edge will set the benchmark for success.

### Getting human and machine relationships right

Nearly 60% of workers now use artificial intelligence intentionally at work, according to a recent study by the Melbourne Business School, yet few organizations are intentionally designing for how humans and machines actually interact. Organizations routinely design human-to-human relationships, and increasingly machine-to-machine workflows as well. But many are still designing work for people and technology separately, rather than designing for both together.

This lack of intentionality is leaving many organizations struggling to realize value from AI. While some organizations are seeing results, most aren't realizing a return on their investments at the speed they need. Organizations can't count on cohesive human-AI interactions to happen organically, considering that only 14% of leaders responding to our 2026 Global Human Capital Trends survey say they are adept at shaping those interactions.

The problem, according to recent Deloitte research, is that most organizations (59%) are taking a tech-focused approach to AI. They layer AI onto legacy systems and processes, rather than reimagining how humans and AI interact, collaborate, and make decisions. But in a world where access to AI is rapidly democratizing, technology alone no longer sets organizations apart—people do. It's how people interact with AI through intentional design that can make the difference.

Deloitte research shows that organizations are twice as likely to exceed their return on investment expectations for AI when they prioritize work design, thoughtfully redesigning human and machine interactions and roles. Consider the results when one European telecommunications company added an AI "expert" to customer service without changing roles or workflow and saw a small 5% productivity lift. But dedicating 90% of the full rollout budget to redesigning human-AI interactions—new workflows, trust thresholds, escalation paths, and robust training—unlocked a 30% productivity increase, as agents learned to partner with AI.

Leaders increasingly recognize what's at stake: Sixty-six percent acknowledge that the intentional design of human-AI interaction is important to organizational success. Yet only 6% say they're leading in this area. Our analysis shows that organizations leading the way on intentional design of human-AI interaction are nearly 2.5 times more likely to report better financial results and twice as likely to say they provide meaningful work.

**The scaffolding for intentional interaction design.** Effective human and machine interaction isn't intuitive; it won't happen by accident or default. Organizations should intentionally design human-AI interactions at both the organizationwide macro level (including design principles, governance, and strategy) and the more granular micro level (specific interactions for particular work, workers, and teams). For design to succeed at both levels, it needs to consider both hardwiring (formal elements like redesigned roles, accountability, decision rights, and clear escalation protocols) and softwiring (informal elements such as leadership behaviors, culture, and psychological safety).

At the macro level, starting with a clear strategic ambition of the desired human and business outcomes is foundational. Michael Ehret, senior vice president and chief people officer at Walmart International, highlights how the company brings outcome-driven and human-centered design principles to life: "We design the way our people work with AI so that it provides an outcome. Too many organizations treat AI as an adoption problem without first asking how you can achieve the outcomes desired. What's really required is behavioral change—not technical training." While 56% of surveyed leaders say they are designing primarily for business outcomes such as cost or speed, a growing number of leaders (40%) are designing for both business and human outcomes. To move beyond traditional silos, some organizations are adopting cross-functional governance models: Moderna has merged IT and HR; Skillsoft's AI council enables cross-functional oversight; and Disney's chief AI and collaboration officer focuses on enabling better collaboration.

Design principles to begin with include being outcome-driven, contextual, transparent, adaptive, human-centered, and empowering. Save the Children demonstrates how choices around trust can accelerate adoption: by building a culture of curiosity and clear guardrails, it doubled weekly usage (from 36% to 71%), quadrupled complex task application from 10% to 45%, increased guardrail awareness from 42% to 70%, and collaborative learning from 36% to 60%.

At the micro level, leaders need to design interactions optimal for each team and type of worker. When rapid AI automation threatened to make 7-Eleven's specialized recruiter role obsolete, it used the rollout of "Rita"—an AI assistant automating 95% of routine hiring tasks and freeing 40,000 hours weekly—to redesign the role rather than replace it. Recruiters shifted from transactional tasks to a strategic focus on enabling store leaders. Workers can interact with AI in a variety of ways—supervising AI's work, being directed by AI as "boss," or working with AI as a muse, thought partner, mentor, or performance coach. MetLife chose AI as a coach for its call center workers, using AI-powered real-time coaching tools to help staff navigate emotionally charged calls—boosting customer satisfaction by 13%, reducing call times, and lowering associate stress. Its Thrive Resets innovation uses AI to monitor associate stress and prompt personalized recovery breaks.

Each interaction type calls for distinct approaches to hardwiring and softwiring. Organizations should be aware of hidden consequences—shifting routine tasks to AI often leaves humans with more complex, demanding work, and increased reliance on AI can lead to worker isolation. Intentionally designing human and machine relationships does more than create efficiency; it opens new frontiers for value creation, human flourishing, and organizational resilience. The future may reward not the fastest adopters, but the most intentional designers.

### Fact or fabrication? AI is blurring the line when it comes to people and work

Organizations are losing trust in data about workers and work at the very moment they depend on it most. For decades, data has been a fundamental substrate of modern organizations. But data without trust has the potential to do more harm than good. Generative AI and agentic systems can now produce content at a staggering scale, potentially blurring authorship, amplifying bias, and creating echo chambers. According to SEO firm Graphite, as of May 2025, more than half of new web articles were generated primarily by AI, up from just 5% before ChatGPT; though 86% of top-ranking Google pages are still human-written, this synthetic wave could contaminate data quality.

According to Deloitte's 2026 survey, only 5% of organizations are making great progress in addressing the decline in quality and trustworthiness of work and workforce data. To address these challenges, organizations should consider expanding beyond cybersecurity to disinformation security and establishing a digital trust pact.

**The coming AI storm** erodes authenticity, agency, and critical judgment. In talent acquisition, 95% of executives are concerned about the accuracy of data on candidates' skills and capabilities—and just over a third of workers admit they regularly use AI to embellish their personal profiles. AI can fabricate entire candidates with deepfaked interviews; one security firm exposed a deepfake by asking the candidate to wave a hand in front of their face, which the bot could not execute. Gartner projects that by 2028, one in four job seekers could be artificial. Some organizations, including Google, are considering bringing back in-person interviews. The rise of ghost jobs—in 2024, 4 in 10 organizations posted jobs with no intention to hire (Resume Builder)—compounds the problem. AI-driven deception has enabled multimillion-dollar frauds, including cybercriminals using deepfake technology to pose as a CFO in a video conference and convincing a worker to pay out $25 million. Nearly half (48%) of executives worry AI may introduce misinformation directly into company datasets.

On agency: 41% say they have used AI to automate part of their job, often without employer awareness, and 80% of executives are concerned that workers are using AI to appear more productive than they are. On judgment: 42% of executives are concerned about employees becoming overly dependent on AI for essential cognitive tasks. Two major risks emerge: "workslop" (passable but shallow outputs that mask weak reasoning and contaminate training sets) and the "AI echo chamber" (tools mirroring a user's past inputs, narrowing perspectives into digital groupthink).

**Disinformation security: Creating a new digital trust pact.** Organizations should safeguard the integrity of worker-related data through practices including AI lineage mapping (tracking data origin, transformation, and use—strengthened by blockchain; SkillsFuture Singapore issues tamper-proof digital certifications), AI risk simulations (stress tests and red-teaming; Identifi Global lets HR simulate deepfake candidates), and real-time dynamic identity authentication. When cybersecurity company Pindrop investigated, it discovered 1 in 6 job applications in its own organization showed clear signs of fraud, with many candidates using deepfakes during live interviews; it developed continuous authentication tools.

**Judgment calls: Helping workers understand what's real.** Technical tools remain probabilistic, not deterministic. Organizations can educate hiring managers and recruiters, train for reflexivity and judgment, and promote transparency in work outputs. Autodesk created AI transparency cards modeled after nutrition labels, and one large pharmaceutical company is experimenting with labeling emails and slide decks by how much content was produced by humans versus AI. Distrust in work and workforce data will likely persist; the real challenge is preserving meaning, authorship, and accountability in an age of synthetic intelligence, with humans as the ultimate stewards of interpretation and judgment.

### AI and the future of human decision-making

A tech company launches an AI résumé screener that quietly learns past biases; a retail service bot makes promises the company doesn't want to keep; clinicians lean on a condition-alert tool that speeds treatment but degrades their ability to spot nuances; an industrial manufacturer puts AI on the board only to learn it could be manipulated. These stories are not science fiction. AI has the potential to transform human decision-making—but organizations should first treat this process as a strategic discipline and then design human–machine decision-making relationships accordingly.

Leaders today face a torrent of choices in conditions noisier, faster, and riskier than ever. In a 2023 Oracle study, 85% of business leaders regret or question decisions they had made in the past, and 72% say the volume of data and their lack of trust in data has stopped them from making any decision at all. Many are turning to AI: in our 2026 survey, 60% of executives now regularly use AI to support their decisions, and Gartner projects that by 2027, half of business decisions will be augmented or automated by AI agents. But AI use may be racing ahead of oversight, bringing distinct challenges around chains of responsibility, ownership, speed and scale, manager preparedness, ethical frameworks, insurability, and regulation. Nearly two-thirds (64%) of respondents consider AI-and-decision-making very important to their current success, but only 5% consider themselves leading the way. Deloitte's High Impact Decision Intelligence research finds more than half of organizations (57%) operate at low decision-making maturity.

**Elevating the discipline of decision-making.** Organizations can use decision frameworks to classify choices and pre-assign owners, data, guardrails, and speed. Amazon's one-way vs. two-way door model helps teams make reversible bets quickly while giving irreversible moves higher scrutiny. Design for decision rigor and integrity by surfacing the decisions that matter (MIT's intelligent choice architectures) and encouraging a sound decision basis and culture. Design for decision rights and governance by modernizing decision rights for AI—Atlassian treats decision rights as something that evolves rather than a rigid rulebook. On governance, Deloitte research finds engagement limited: in 2024, nearly half (45%) of surveyed board members and C-suite executives said AI is not on the agenda, and only 14% discussed it every meeting; in 2025, 40% say AI has caused them to think differently about their boards' makeup. IBM uses dedicated ethics boards, and regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act are requiring clear AI oversight mechanisms.

**Elevating both humans and machines as decision makers.** Organizations should design to grow decision-making skills (BAE Systems' case-based learning; DBS Bank's PURE principles and iGrow platform) and to evaluate AI performance—monitoring AI behavior continuously (Spotify defined human standards for podcast summaries before automating evaluation), creating anchors for accountability, and being aware of cognitive asymmetry (the EU's TechDispatch cautions that a human-in-the-loop does not guarantee safety; some enterprises use "guardian agents").

**Elevating human agency.** People more readily accept responsibility when they feel real influence. Liberty Mutual enables claims adjusters to override AI's suggestions; as one leader said, "The moment AI enters the workflow, the real question isn't 'What does the model say?' It's 'Who gets to disagree with it, and how fast?'" Stanford researchers introduced a framework matching AI agent autonomy to the degree of human agency desired. On trust, Deloitte's Trustworthy AI research shows workers who trust the AI agents they work with are 10 times more likely to see those agents as critical to creating value; people extend trust when technology demonstrates reliability, capability, transparency, and humanity. Organizations that elevate decision-making as a discipline can gain speed and quality without sacrificing trust—the path to AI as a trusted adviser while keeping humans firmly in charge of the "why."

### Dealing with AI's cultural debt

In moments of uncertainty when cultural coherence may matter most, organizations often struggle to maintain it. This neglect can cause organizations to develop "cultural debt"—the negative consequences accumulated by neglecting culture, similar to how financial debt accrues interest. Much of the organizational focus of AI disruption centers on how workers interact with AI rather than how AI impacts the human-to-human interactions that shape culture. In fact, 42% of workers report that their organization rarely evaluates the impact of AI on people—an indicator of mounting cultural debt.

Culture is built on trust, and AI is breaking that trust: 80% of leaders, managers, and workers are concerned their co-workers and teams are using AI to appear more productive than they are. Workers are quietly navigating new questions: Is it cheating if I use AI to do my work? What is hard work if AI is doing the heavy lifting? Who is to blame if AI is wrong? If I don't use AI, will I lose my job—or will AI take my job anyway?

**The cultural costs of AI.** AI is reshaping work—increased workloads and stress, reduced well-being, higher loneliness, decreased autonomy. On Wall Street alone, analysts forecast AI and automation will eliminate up to 200,000 jobs by 2028 or 2030 (Forbes), and the World Economic Forum reports 41% of employers globally plan to reduce their workforce due to skills obsolescence by 2030. A 2025 Gallup poll found only 20% of US workers feel strongly connected to their company's culture, and Edelman's Trust Barometer found trust in employers declined in 2025 for the first time since 2018. The good news: 34% of organizations recognize culture as a direct inhibitor to their AI transformation goals, and 65% believe their culture needs to change significantly. Yet while just over half felt the impact of AI on culture was important, only 5% are making great progress.

**From cultural debt to culture as an asset.** Organizations should set a strong foundation (leadership alignment—Cisco research found employees are twice as likely to use AI if their leaders do; anchoring on purpose and values; embedding ethical AI use—IBM's AI ethics board; open communication—Highmark Health's Marcia Oglan: "Tech won't solve trust issues. Only visible, consistent leadership and accountability can do that"). They should build trust and human connection in the flow of work (Trek Bicycle identified nearly 40 concrete AI use cases prioritizing well-being; Cisco's Team Space tool; DBS Bank's recognition and reward points and transformation days). And they can use AI to promote healthy cultures: Atlassian uses AI agents to improve onboarding, seeing a jump from 57% to 93% in average weekly AI usage among new hires; ScultureAI coaches workers toward core values; and Workhuman's human intelligence functionality mines recognition data. Clinging to the cultural status quo is not a neutral choice—organizations that shape and deploy culture to harness AI's potential are likely to drive better outcomes.

### The orchestration advantage

Competitive advantage today depends on more than what organizations own; increasingly, it depends on how they can steer intent into action, fluidly reconfiguring capabilities and capacity as conditions shift. Some 67% of leaders say their primary competitive advantage over the next three years will come from being fast and nimble, while only 28% believe scale will be their main differentiator. AI is upending traditional assumptions around capability, capacity, and the classic speed-quality-cost triangle, creating a new performance frontier where all three can improve simultaneously. It's the difference between humans *plus* machines and humans *times* machines.

The key is not just allocating resources into fixed structures, but the ability to fluidly orchestrate people, skills, data and technologies around business-critical outcomes—continuously sensing, assembling, and recombining. Allocation is assigning a musician to play a part; orchestration is the conductor's role. Levi Strauss increased sales in its loose fit jeans category by 15% in three months by rapidly bringing together designers, merchants, and marketing with AI to sense demand and iterate quickly. Organizations leading in orchestration are about twice as likely as peers to report better financial results and meaningful work. The ability to dynamically orchestrate work ranks No. 1 among trends of importance this year, with 88% of leaders saying it is extremely or very important, yet only 7% say they are making great progress—an 81-point gap, the largest in this year's survey.

**Four actions to orchestrate capability and capacity.** First, identify and create capability and capacity via build, borrow, buy, and now "bot" (AI): 56% of leaders now organize and evaluate AI agents as digital workers, and 60% say their teams have the right human and AI capabilities. Just over half (51%) account for human-machine collaboration when planning workforce size. Of the one-third of leaders who exited workers due to AI, more than half (57%) have come to question their decision—one fintech that claimed its chatbot could do the work of 700 representatives is now rehiring people to work with AI. Only 50% of workers and managers say their organizations are tapping into their full potential. Cleveland Clinic's role redesign shifted most medical-assistant tasks (37 of 40) to lower-credentialed or non-clinical staff, creating capacity equivalent to 430 full-time employees and more than $2 million in cost savings. Only 28% of organizations currently use dynamic teams, but 59% say doing so will be important.

Second, place the right decisions with the right people at the right time—Walmart uses a cross-functional leadership model; Hewlett Packard Enterprise integrated its strategic workforce planning and organization design teams; Mastercard, Seagate, and Standard Chartered use an orchestration platform to integrate skills, tasks, and AI. Only 11% of managers strongly agree their organization provides relevant data and tools for work-distribution decisions, though 61% now align and deploy workers based on tasks, skills, and outcomes vs. 33% using job- or position-based models. Third, create plug-and-play modularity: 60% of leaders are working to redeploy internal capabilities and 62% say they are adept at plugging in external capabilities. Fourth, use AI to help orchestrate—58% of leaders prefer to shape strategy through frequent experimentation vs. 37% through centralized planning. Only 15% of organizations currently use digital twins, but 49% say they will be important; only 20% currently use AI to monitor workforce-change signals, though 52% say it will be important. The future may belong not to the best planners, but the best orchestrators.

### Have organizational functions outlived their function?

A CEO preparing a major product expansion needs an integrated financial, workforce, and supply chain plan, but functional capacity is limited, capability is mismatched, and end-to-end data and processes are lacking—while functional headcounts and leadership layers have expanded, adding cost without enabling growth. The functional pillars that have long been foundational increasingly feel outdated. In Deloitte's 2026 survey, 66% of C-suite leaders agree it is very or extremely important to push beyond the boundaries of traditional organizational functions, but only 7% are making great progress.

**Functions under pressure.** Organizations are under constant pressure to rein in the cost of corporate functions while increasing speed to value. In the last three years, headcounts in HR, sales and support, and business management have shrunk, and US public companies have cut white-collar workforces by 3.5%. Over half of organizations using a global business services model include finance, HR, IT, and procurement in scope for shared services, and 58% expect to increase that footprint; more than half of organizations with a global business service leader role reported savings of more than 20%. As work gets automated, augmented, or consolidated, domain experts increasingly sit outside the core value chain and must broaden their expertise around business problems.

**Getting intentional about the future of functions.** Rather than tweaking at the margins, leaders should reimagine functions by breaking them down and building them up in new ways. The first step is to separate "run the business" activities (repeatable processes with substantial automation potential, addressable through global business services) from "grow the business" activities (linked by the business situations they support, requiring agile cross-functional teams). Areas of opportunity include: following data and tech across functional boundaries (93% of IT leaders believe HR-IT integrations can boost productivity, satisfaction, and engagement per Nexthink; Moderna merged HR and IT under a chief people and digital technology officer and deployed more than 3,000 tailored ChatGPT versions; Unilever gave its CFO responsibility for supply chain, procurement, digital and technology, and business services); decoupling domain expertise from organizational structure; enhancing cross-functional accountability and collaboration; and enabling new capabilities and insights in the business (building on boundaryless HR from the 2024 report). Leaders must be clear-eyed about human tendencies to protect turf and political power while preserving essential expertise and stewardship. Functions may have outlived their function—organizations have the opportunity to arrange themselves for greater fluidity, agility, and cohesiveness.

### Staying relevant in a world that won't sit still

Organizations and workers alike are striving to adapt to the ever-increasing pace of change. Change management and corporate training have traditionally been the go-to tools, but they rarely evolve fast enough. Deloitte's 2026 survey found only 27% of respondents believe their organizations manage change effectively, and only 8% believe their organizations are highly effective at meeting the continuous, "always-on" learning needs of their workforce. AI is flipping the script—enabling workers to sense, practice, and apply new ways of doing things directly in the flow of work. The words *change management* and *training* may now be outdated; we need a new vocabulary focused on growth and adaptiveness.

Creating an adaptive experience ranked as the second most important trend this year, with 85% saying it is critical to develop adaptability at the speed required—however, only 74% say they are making any progress, and just 7% say they are leading. One-third of workers experienced 15 major changes in the past year alone. The steady cadence of change has led to decreased well-being (68%), increased workload (60%), and feeling less relevant or left behind (58%). An intentional, more empathetic approach is needed—changing the narrative from "change exhaustion" to "changefulness," cultivating workers' abilities to adapt, experiment, learn, and evolve as a daily muscle. Organizations that successfully cultivate this adaptive approach are 2.4 times more likely to report better financial results and provide more meaningful work.

**Four approaches to adaptiveness and growth.** First, create a surround-sound system—an omnichannel experience embedding adaptive experiences in the work itself (one global pharma company forgoing its traditional change management playbook uses in-app guidance, AI-powered adoption agents, and behavioral nudges, expecting 95% of employees to adopt a new CRM confidently without any formal training). Second, shift to change and learning at the unit of one—AI can hyper-personalize by identifying behaviors, modeling impact on segments, providing real-time feedback, and tailoring to motivations (Georgia-Pacific uses AI-personalized videos and communications; Plum predicts how a team will react to change). The top challenge cited by leaders and managers is change not seeming relevant or personalized (44%); worker barriers include not understanding how the change relates to them (30%) and lacking personal motivation (21%). Human-centric personalization makes organizations nearly three times as likely to report better business and human outcomes. Third, create continuous feedback loops (one pharma company uses AI to surface real-time insights from sentiment and behavior; Nike uses data analytics to identify adoption barriers; Toyota Memorial Hospital links learning and Kaizen to care outcomes). Fourth, empower workers to sense and respond—87% of workers say "digital playgrounds" (safe digital spaces to practice) will help them adapt, and workers are twice as likely to feel negatively about an employer-imposed change than a self-imposed one (Walmart lets associates test stocking and staffing models; Unilever's AI labs enable experimentation). The greatest source of competitive advantage may be the ability to organically adapt in real time—shifting from control to curiosity, from training to experimentation.

### Decisions that echo

We are living in a moment when confidence in institutions—public and private—is eroding at an unprecedented pace. The 2025 Edelman Trust Barometer finds that 61% of people globally now feel a moderate or high sense of belief that government and business make their lives harder and serve narrow interests, and only 36% believe the next generation will be better off. According to HP's 2025 Work Relationship Index, only 16% of knowledge workers trust their senior leaders to make the right decisions for their people—a double-digit drop in one year. A recent Wall Street Journal poll shows 70% of Americans believe the "American Dream" was never real at all. These are signals that the relationship between people, institutions, and work is under strain—and that the decisions of leaders, organizations, and boards now reverberate far beyond corporate walls.

**The expanding mandate of the board.** The statistics are evidence that leaders and boards are quickly reaching a tipping point. This is why human sustainability—the degree to which an organization creates value for people as human beings—matters so deeply now. Traditionally, boards have focused on strategy, leadership and talent, financial performance, risk and compliance, governance, and stakeholder engagement. But boards should have heightened awareness that any decision can impact broader societal outcomes: health and well-being, labor market health, truth and trust, and general economic health.

**The decisions ahead.** For each trend, boards face an immediate question and a longer-term consideration: On human and machine relationships (Today: How do we realize returns on AI investments? Long-term: without intentional design, organizations can hollow out empathy, nuance, and contextual judgment). On disinformation (protecting data vs. preserving institutional legitimacy and a shared foundation of truth). On decision-making (defining accountability vs. extending human capability without surrendering agency). On cultural debt (having the right culture vs. AI hollowing out belonging, creativity, and civic cohesion). On orchestration (having the necessary talent and capacity vs. becoming fast followers without purpose). On functions (meeting business needs at lower cost vs. avoiding bureaucratic confusion and loss of expertise). On adaptable workforce (having leadership and workforce capabilities vs. widening skill gaps and social exclusion).

**Near-term actions for long-term impact.** Two foundational questions will shape the future of work for years: How will value and opportunity be distributed among individuals and organizations? What is our human advantage—what remains uniquely human and how will we preserve and elevate it? Boards can ensure executive teams have what they need to act on immediate questions, actively engage with long-term considerations as a lens for all decisions, and extend influence beyond organizational boundaries through consortia, community partnerships, educational alliances, and public-sector collaboration. The future will likely be defined not by the technology we adopt, but by the judgment, values, and courage we bring to the decisions ahead. The human advantage remains real and irreplaceable—and that work begins with the choices we make today.

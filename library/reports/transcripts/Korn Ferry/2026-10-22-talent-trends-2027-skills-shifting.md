## Full report
### Talent Trends 2027: Skills-Shifting
In 2027, the workforce will be defined by what people can do, not the jobs they hold. 
That's because the skills businesses need will shift more than they'll stick. And those shifts will happen faster than ever.
People might stay in the same organization, often in the same role, even as the skills they need change. Careers will start differently and move in new directions. 
And talent teams and managers will reshape their own ways of working in this new fluid landscape. 
The advantage will come from how quickly people, teams, and organizations can learn, adapt, and put new skills to work.  
Korn Ferry’s 13th annual report reveals the seven talent trends shaping the year ahead for talent teams and leaders.

### Trend 1: The Shape-Shifting Job
In 2027, a job won’t sit still anymore. The role someone signed up for and the one they’ll be doing might be two different jobs wearing the same title.
At such a swift pace, capability mismatch is bound to follow. The skills a job will need a year or two from now are unlikely to be the skills people have when they start. Workers may struggle to keep up with how fast that target moves.
“Talent leaders have seen this coming, but it’s really picking up speed,” says David Ellis of Korn Ferry. “They’re asking, ‘Do our people have the skills for the work that's coming next?’”
That’s why the most forward-thinking are prioritizing workforce-wide upskilling and reskilling as a matter of urgency.
The good news is that most employees will be just as eager to build new skills as their companies are to teach them.
In fact, in our Workforce 2026 survey, 65% of workers said they would stay in their job if it offered learning and development opportunities—making this trend a win-win for everyone.
### Lead the Shift: Your Next Steps
Knowing what the business needs a year from now is hard. Start by mapping where your strategy and technology are heading, then use a skills assessment framework to find the gaps and close them.
Map tomorrow's skills needs
Performance reviews, project history, and manager notes can reveal the next skill a person may be ready to develop. Use that insight to target reskilling at the individual level, not just a company-wide push.
Uncover reskilling potential
Prioritize your training and development plan to focus on the most crucial skills for business needs.
Develop the critical skills

### Trend 2: Wanted: Adaptability
The year's most-wanted skill will be adaptability—the ability to keep learning and shifting to whatever the job needs next. 
Adaptability shows up in small course corrections more than in big pivots, in the calls people make when there's no clear answer yet. “It's that real-time judgment that talent leaders are really after,” says Jeanne MacDonald of Korn Ferry. 
But being adaptable only works when it’s part of the full package of soft skills, including 2026’s top skill, critical thinking, as well as collaboration. 
“Collaboration is becoming one of those skills we're not talking about, but it’s crucial for leaders who want to drive change,” says Cathi Rittelmann of Korn Ferry. People adapt more quickly when they don’t have to figure everything out alone. 
Leaders need to create the conditions for adaptability, building a culture where people are encouraged to ask questions and work through change together.

### Lead the Shift: Your Next Steps
Many companies still judge adaptability by gut feel, but it’s measurable. Korn Ferry defines it as learning agility, the ability to learn fast and apply that learning to something new. Treat adaptability as a testable capability to ensure you’re getting what you need. “Knowing when to trust instinct, when to reach for data, and when to blend the two—that's the real test,” says Cathi Rittelmann.
Assess, don't guess
Like any skill, adaptability will grow through exposure. Move people into roles, projects, or teams outside, but adjacent, to what they already know, and let the unfamiliar do the teaching.
Stretch it out
Embed a have-a-go mentality into your culture. If someone takes a risk and gets punished for it, everyone will learn to stick to what they're good at and play it safe. It would be better to publicly reward people when they try out a new approach or skill, even if it doesn’t always lead to a roaring success.
Reward risk-takers

### Trend 3: Entry-Level Upgrade
Junior roles have been widely reported as first on the chopping block, the easiest for AI to replace.
That’s shifting in 2027. A majority of businesses plan to grow or maintain their entry-level headcount, redesigning those starter jobs instead of cutting them.
AI will be central to that redesign. It can handle repetitive work faster than humans, so tasks that used to fill someone’s first year, such as entering data and building reports, will be handled by bots. 
That will free up new recruits for higher-value work. In one role, that might mean judging what AI delivers, where it’s right and what it gets wrong. In another, it might be talking to a client directly or managing a real account earlier than in the past. 
"Companies are already building fast, three-month paths to get high schoolers job-ready—and that pipeline is going to grow," says MacDonald.

### Lead the Shift: Your Next Steps
Give candidates a real task from the job itself, such as a sample of AI output to judge or a mock client scenario to handle, and see how they perform. Keep the test short or offer a fee if it’s a lengthy test with an output you might actually use.
A fair test
Judgment isn't something people figure out alone. Let new hires shadow someone senior handling a task before they're expected to take on something similar themselves.
A helping hand
New hires should be given a small project with clear objectives early on. Not a shadow assignment. Give them something with actual stakes.
Make it real
Higher-value work is exciting, right up until someone's underwater in month two. Check in earlier than you think you should so you can give them some guidance if needed.
Check in and step in

### Trend 4: Fluid Paths
For decades, a career moved in a straight line. Straight up a ladder, rung by rung. 
But in 2027, workers will zigzag more than they climb, landing in places no succession plan accounted for. 
It will take a broader range of skills to rise through the ranks, so people will be searching for ways to learn them. 
That’s why people will increasingly take charge of their own career path. Instead of working towards the next rung on the ladder, they might prioritize moving to a new team or a job that will teach them different skills. 
“Traditional layers won't necessarily be the motivator anymore,” says Radhika Papandreou of Korn Ferry. “It will be about the skills you need next, not the job you want next.” 
For talent leaders, that raises a difficult question. How do you plan a pipeline when people are more likely to sidestep through different parts of the business than to climb straight up?
### Lead the Shift: Your Next Steps
A short list of two or three names in a succession pipeline assumes everyone still moves straight up. Most people won't anymore.
Widen the pool
Recognize skill-building and cross-functional experience in pay and recognition. Treat them as real progress, not a detour.
Reward lateral moves
When career paths become less predictable, recruiters need to be part of the conversation earlier. “The question isn't just, ‘Do we have the people?’ It's, ‘Do our people have the skills for the work that's coming?’" says Ellis.
Bring TA in early
A title tells you what someone was hired to do. It doesn't tell you what they can do now or what they could do next. Track skills development instead, so you know exactly who to tap when a critical role opens.
Map skills, not titles

### Trend 5: Managers Without Maps
A manager’s job used to be about managing people’s work—keeping them on track and making sure they had what they needed to deliver.
The job has already been broadening, but in 2027, a substantial shift is coming. Managers will spend more time guiding teams through change than managing their day-to-day work. 
AI adoption will make that harder. Just 6% of respondents believe their senior leaders are fully prepared to guide their organizations through AI-driven transformation. 
And that uncertainty then lands with managers. They’re the ones who have to translate evolving AI strategies into clear direction for their teams, explaining what’s changing and how it will impact their work. 
“Managers will become the shock absorbers of the organization,” says Rittelmann. “Their job will be to help teams keep moving, even when they can’t tell them exactly where things are heading.”
### Lead the Shift: Your Next Steps
Leadership training is often built around running a stable team. Create training programs that simulate major changes to the business, so managers can practice leading their teams through ongoing disruption.
Train for change
Assess managers on how quickly they can adapt when circumstances shift. Those who respond well are more likely to help their teams do the same.
Look for agility
When senior leaders make decisions about AI or other fast-moving issues, managers need more context than just the decision. Change won’t happen until mindsets shift, and that only happens when people can understand the bigger picture.
Give managers the context
Managers are usually expected to explain why change is happening, but when the goalposts keep moving, that won’t always be possible. Coach managers to become comfortable with not always having the answer.
Make room for "I don't know"

### Trend 6: Trading Skills
Electricians, technicians, machine operators, welders—the people who keep physical operations running—will be even harder to find in 2027 as demand for their skills soars. 
Years of career advice have pushed people toward degrees and away from hands-on work. Trade school enrollments dropped. Apprenticeship pipelines shrank. 
But as infrastructure and energy projects expand, there won’t be enough people who can do the hands-on work needed to deliver them. 
For knowledge workers, it's a different story. Their opportunities will shrink faster than frontline workers because AI is aimed squarely at the kind of work they do. 
That’s why so many governments are planning major investments in apprenticeships and technical training. They want to encourage more people to learn the skills needed in fields where talent will be in short supply. 
“Those pipelines will take time to rebuild, leaving a gap between supply and demand that will give tradespeople and frontline workers a strong advantage,” says Papandreou.
How is demand for talent changing in 2027?
Traditional assumptions about “future-proof” jobs are being challenged. (81%)
Organizations will face increasing shortages of skilled manual and technical labor. (65%)
AI will disrupt white-collar and knowledge-based work more than frontline work. (64%)
Demand for skilled trades and frontline workers will grow faster than demand for knowledge workers. (53%)
We are seeing a long-term shift toward infrastructure, manufacturing, energy, and industrial investment. (51%)
### Lead the Shift: Your Next Steps
People might be less inclined to take on debt for a trade the way they might for a degree. Offer to cover the cost upfront and watch your applicant pool expand.
Pay for their certifications
The best teacher for a technician role is someone already doing the job well. Reward them for the hours they spend training someone new.
Reward your in-house trainers
Shortages are worse in places nobody wants to relocate to. "If a plant or facility goes up somewhere remote, the technicians you need often won't exist there, and the ones you want are unlikely to want to move," says MacDonald. "So you’ll need to train up the talent that's already in the area."
Build local talent
Job listings for frontline roles often make it sound like they’re placeholder jobs, even when the role could lead to something bigger. When writing up the descriptions, focus on what they could be—a competitive career with a solid future.
Advertise trade jobs like office jobs

### Trend 7: Talent Teams Connect the Dots
Hiring, learning, and workforce planning currently operate as separate departments, each with its own leader and budget. That's changing. As skills become more fluid, those silos will start to break down in 2027.
The skills a business hires for, builds internally, and needs next are all part of the same workforce puzzle. HR will become a more united team, bringing those decisions together rather than managing them separately. 
“Talent teams will join forces to become a single talent ecosystem. Everyone will sit at the same table,” says Ellis. 
This all-in-one HR team will also gain some new senior roles. Look out for new positions with unique responsibilities, such as Head of Skills, Head of Work Design, and Head of Human-AI.

As long as every function controls its own budget, it'll keep making separate decisions, no matter what the reporting lines say. Combine the money, and more connected decision-making will follow.
Merge the money
The fastest way to bring these functions together will be through who you hire next. "The companies ahead of the curve on this merger will be the ones who recruit for an enterprise leadership mindset into HR—people who think about the whole business, not just their corner of it," notes Papandreou.
Recruit broad thinkers
TA, L&D, and workforce planning shape a person's hiring, growth, and career path from different angles. Map where those processes overlap and where the real gaps are, then use that to decide what belongs in the new HR function.
Map the gaps and overlaps
If you’re planning to add a new senior role, such as Head of Skills, but you don’t give them authority to make decisions, then it’ll just be a title, not a job. Back it with real responsibility.

### Frequently Asked Questions
What are the most important talent trends for 2027?
The defining talent trend for 2027 will be that skills needs are changing faster than ever, redefining all aspects of work. 
Organizations will increasingly focus on what people can do and how quickly they can learn, rather than relying solely on fixed jobs and titles. 
Other major trends include: 
adaptability becoming a priority skill 
entry-level roles being redesigned around higher-value work 
career paths will become less linear 
demand for skilled trades will grow 
managers will coach people more on change management than managing day-to-day workloads 
closer integration across HR, talent acquisition, learning, and workforce planning
How can organizations prepare for changing workforce skills?
Organizations should begin by connecting business and technology strategy to the capabilities they will need next. A skills assessment can then identify gaps between current and future requirements. 
Performance reviews, project histories, and manager insights can help reveal employees’ reskilling potential, while targeted development can build the most critical capabilities. 
This approach supports faster redeployment and helps organizations decide when to develop existing employees and when external recruitment is necessary.
Why will adaptability be a critical workforce skill in 2027?
Adaptability will matter because jobs, technology and organizational needs will continue to shift. It involves learning quickly, applying knowledge in unfamiliar situations, and making sound decisions when the answer is not yet clear. 
Employers can assess adaptability through learning agility rather than relying on instinct alone. You can also develop adaptability by giving employees exposure to adjacent roles, projects and teams, and by encouraging experimentation. 
How will AI change entry-level jobs and early careers? 
AI will increasingly handle routine activities, such as data entry and report production, but this does not mean entry-level work will disappear. 
Instead, many organizations expect to retain, grow, or redesign early-career roles so that entry-level employees can take on higher-value responsibilities sooner. These could include evaluating AI-generated work, interacting with clients and exercising judgement. 
Employers will need realistic skills assessments, structured support, and meaningful early assignments to help new hires build these capabilities.
How should HR change as organizations become more skills-based?
HR will need to connect talent acquisition, learning and development, workforce planning, and internal mobility around a shared view of skills. 
Instead of making separate hiring, development, and succession decisions, these teams should map capability gaps and overlaps together. This could require integrated budgets, broader leadership profiles and senior roles with responsibility for skills or work design. 
Giving these roles genuine decision-making authority will be essential if organizations want a unified workforce strategy rather than additional organizational complexity.
How is talent acquisition changing in 2027?
As our Talent Trends 2027 research shows, organizations are placing greater emphasis on skills, learning agility, and long-term potential rather than relying solely on job titles, degrees, and traditional career paths. 
Recruiters will increasingly assess what candidates can do now and how quickly they can develop new skills for the future. 
As jobs continue to evolve, talent acquisition teams will work more closely with workforce planning, learning and development, and HR leaders to identify emerging skill needs and build future-ready talent pipelines. 
Organizations will also place greater value on candidates who can adapt to changing business needs and take on higher-value work as technology reshapes roles.
What does skills-based hiring mean for talent acquisition leaders?
Skills-based hiring shifts the focus from credentials, job titles, and career history towards demonstrated capabilities. 
Rather than asking where someone worked or what degree they hold, organizations assess what they can do and how quickly they can learn. 
This approach can expand talent pools, improve internal mobility, and help organisations hire for emerging roles where traditional experience might not yet exist.

### Press release (22 October 2026)
Korn Ferry Report: AI and Capability Gaps Will Reshape Talent in 2027
Firm’s Annual Talent Trends Report Ranks Adaptability the No. 1 Workforce Skill
Los Angeles, October 22, 2026 – New research from Korn Ferry (NYSE: KFY) finds that AI and widening gaps between the skills organizations have and the capabilities they need will reshape how talent is hired, developed and deployed in 2027. 
“The work people are being asked to do is evolving faster than many organizations are prepared for,” said Jeanne MacDonald, Chief Executive Officer, Recruitment Process Outsourcing, Korn Ferry. “The organizations that understand the skills they have, anticipate what they will need next and develop people quickly will be best positioned to thrive in 2027 and beyond.”
More than three-quarters (77%) of leaders surveyed say capability gaps will be a greater challenge than talent shortages, while 78% say too few people are prepared for how work will be done in the future.
Next year’s most-wanted skill, the research found, will be adaptability. Being adaptable to what jobs require next is further amplified when coupled with critical thinking and collaboration, which are ranked the second and third most-desired skills in the survey.
The findings, based on a survey of more than 1,300 talent and senior leaders across more than 75 countries, point to seven workforce shifts:
Adaptability is the No. 1 workforce skill. Organizations will need to assess, develop and reward it as a core workforce capability.
AI will redefine entry-level work. Sixty-three percent expect AI agents to take over routine tasks, while 40% plan to maintain entry-level hiring but with a greater focus on higher-value work.
Skills will matter more than roles. Sixty-five percent say work will be organized around skills, 67% expect people to move across projects and 66% plan to invest heavily in reskilling.
Career paths will become more fluid. Ninety-one percent expect employees to take greater ownership of development, and 87% say lateral moves will become as important as promotions.
Managers will need stronger people skills. Ninety-one percent say coaching and people skills will become more important. And 66% expect managers to spend more time guiding their teams through change than managing their day-to-day work.
Technical labor shortages will grow. Sixty-five percent anticipate shortages of skilled manual and technical labor, while 64% expect AI to cause more disruption in knowledge work than in frontline work.
Talent functions will become more integrated. Sixty-nine percent expect hiring, learning and development, and workforce planning teams to work more closely together, supported by emerging roles such as head of skills, head of work design and head of human-AI.
“Skills-based work, AI-enabled job redesign and more fluid career paths are accelerating,” MacDonald added. “Organizations will need clear career pathways, meaningful development and managers who help people build the capabilities their businesses need.”

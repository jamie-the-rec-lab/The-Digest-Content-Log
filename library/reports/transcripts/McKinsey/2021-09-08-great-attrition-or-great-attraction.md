## Full report
A record number of employees are quitting or thinking about it, and organisations that learn why (and act thoughtfully) gain an edge in attracting and retaining talent. More than 19 million US workers had quit since April 2021, a record pace. Many companies struggle because they do not understand why employees are leaving, and jump to quick fixes like pay bumps or "thank you" bonuses without strengthening the relational ties people have with colleagues and employers. The result is that employees sense a transaction, not appreciation.

Employees crave investment in the human aspects of work. They are tired, many are grieving, and they want a renewed sense of purpose, social and interpersonal connection, and shared identity. They want pay, benefits and perks, but more than that they want to feel valued and to have meaningful interactions rather than transactions. By failing to invest in a more fulfilling employee experience and to meet new demands for autonomy and flexibility, some employers are pushing employees to withdraw entirely from traditional full-time employment.

The Great Attrition is happening, is widespread, and is likely to persist or accelerate. Forty percent of surveyed employees said they were at least somewhat likely to quit in the next three to six months, and 18% ranged from likely to almost certain. Findings held across all five countries surveyed (Australia, Canada, Singapore, the UK and the US) and were broadly consistent across industries. Leisure and hospitality was most at risk, but many healthcare and white-collar workers also planned to quit, and even among educators almost a third said they were at least somewhat likely to. Fifty-three percent of employers reported greater voluntary turnover than in previous years, and 64% expected it to continue or worsen over the next six months.

Attrition could worsen because employees are willing to quit without a job lined up. Thirty-six percent of those who quit in the past six months did so without a new job in hand, a fundamental difference from previous downturn-and-recovery cycles. US employees were most likely to leave without a new job (40%); 42% of healthcare and social-assistance workers who quit did so without one; and a quarter of white-collar quitters had no job lined up. Among those at least somewhat likely to leave in the next three to six months, almost two-thirds would do so without a new job lined up.

Otherwise satisfied employees may also be tempted to quit as options expand. Sixty percent said they were not at all likely to quit, but they should not be considered safe: among them, 65% cited liking where they lived as a primary reason to stay, yet almost 90% of people who took new jobs in new cities in the past six months did not have to relocate because so many employers now allow remote work. More "location agnostic" roles could prompt otherwise satisfied employees to reconsider, especially if leaders mishandle the shift to hybrid work.

Employers cannot fix what they do not understand. When asked why their people quit, employers cited compensation, work-life balance and physical and emotional health. Those mattered, but not as much as employers thought. The top three factors employees cited were that they did not feel valued by their organisation (54%) or their manager (52%), or lacked a sense of belonging at work (51%). Employees who identified as non-White or multiracial were more likely than White counterparts to say they left because they did not feel they belonged. Employees prioritised relational factors; employers focused on transactional ones.

To start turning attrition into attraction, leaders should pause and think through their next moves with employees rather than in a vacuum, because executives are not listening to their people enough. Useful questions: Do we shelter toxic leaders? Do we have the right people in the right places, especially managers (who need new skills for hybrid)? How strong was our culture before the pandemic, given prior weaknesses are now magnified? Is our work environment merely transactional (raising pay alone signals the relationship is only a paycheck)? Are our benefits aligned with employee priorities (45% of leavers cited family care; Patagonia retains nearly 100% of new mothers via on-site childcare)? Can we offer real career paths and development (employees want recognition and progression; some firms create multiple levels within a role)? And how are we building a sense of community (in-person connection still matters but needs careful, inclusive management)?

The Great Attrition is real, will continue, and may worsen before it improves, but it is also an opportunity: by listening, learning and acting on the relational aspects of work people have missed most, employers can turn the Great Attrition into the Great Attraction.

Authors: Aaron De Smet, Bonnie Dowling, Marino Mugayar-Baldocchi and Bill Schaninger (McKinsey People & Organizational Performance).

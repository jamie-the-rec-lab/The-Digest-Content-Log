## Summary
The latest figures provide a mixed picture overall. The employment rate increased and the unemployment rate fell in the latest quarter, though these movements partly reflect particularly large differences in employment status between the groups entering and leaving the survey.
Over the same period, the number of workers on payroll fell. Vacancies also fell further, with the decline again concentrated in smaller businesses, which report that increased labour and operating costs are affecting their hiring decisions.
Meanwhile, regular wage growth slowed, driven by a fall in public sector wage growth, as the recent impact from the timing of pay awards has started to drop out of the numbers.

## Latest indicators at a glance

### Payrolled employees
Estimates for payrolled employees in the UK fell by 67,000 (0.2%) between September 2025 and September 2026, but increased by 20,000 (0.1%) between August and September 2026. This is based on administrative data from HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC).
When looking at July to September 2026, the period comparable with our Labour Force Survey (LFS) estimates, the number of payrolled employees fell by 90,000 (0.3%) over the year and by 32,000 (0.1%) over the quarter.
The early estimate of payrolled employees for October 2026 decreased by 95,000 (0.3%) on the year, and decreased by 34,000 (0.1%) between September and October 2026, to 30.2 million. Figures for October should be treated as provisional estimates and are likely to be revised when more data are received next month.

## Employment
The UK employment rate for people aged 16 to 64 years, including both employees and self-employed, was estimated at 75.4% for July to September 2026. This is up by 0.4 percentage points on the year and up by 0.3 percentage points on the latest quarter.

## Unemployment
The UK unemployment rate for people aged 16 years and over was estimated at 4.6% in July to September 2026. This is down by 0.3 percentage points on both the year and the latest quarter.

## Economic inactivity
The UK economic inactivity rate for people aged 16 to 64 years was estimated at 20.8% in July to September 2026. This is down by 0.1 percentage points on both the year and the latest quarter.
The quarterly movements in employment and unemployment in the latest data partly reflect the rotation of the Labour Force Survey (LFS) sample. More information on this is included in Section 9: Current quality considerations.

### Claimant Count
The UK Claimant Count for October 2026 increased on the month and on the year, to an estimated 1.714 million.
The Claimant Count figure for the latest month is provisional and subject to revisions after first publication. This is the result of later amendments to records in the administrative systems, for example, as work capability assessments conclude and more information is available about benefit claimants' ability to work.

### Vacancies
The estimated number of vacancies in the UK decreased in the latest quarter. Early estimates for August to October 2026 suggest a decrease of 22,000 (3.0%) to 690,000, compared with May to July 2026. This is the largest quarterly fall since May to July 2025, which was a fall of 33,000. On the year, vacancies decreased by 37,000 (5.0%).
Outside of the coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic period, the last time there were 690,000 or fewer vacancies was in July to September 2014, when there were 686,000 vacancies.
Over the quarter, vacancy estimates fell in 11 of the 18 industry sectors. The largest decreases were in administrative and support service activities, construction, and transport and storage, each down by 5,000 vacancies.
Of the five employment size bands, three had decreases in the number of vacancies on the quarter to August to October 2026. The largest quarterly decrease was in businesses with 1 to 9 employees, down by 22,000 to 73,000 vacancies. Outside of the pandemic, this is the lowest level for this employment size band since April to June 2012, when there were also 73,000 vacancies.
Feedback from our Vacancy Survey suggests that smaller firms may not be recruiting because of labour costs and other economic conditions.

### Average weekly earnings
Annual growth in employees’ average regular earnings (excluding bonuses) was 3.5% in July to September 2026, a slight decrease on the previous three-month period, when it was 3.7%.
Annual growth in total earnings (including bonuses) was 3.6% in July to September 2026, down from 3.8% on the previous three-month period. It was last lower than 3.6% in August to October 2020, when it was 2.8%.
Annual average regular earnings growth was 5.4% for the public sector and 3.1% for the private sector. Public sector annual pay growth continues to be affected by variations in the timing of pay awards this year, but this base effect is reducing. More information on this can be found in our Average weekly earnings in Great Britain: November 2026 bulletin.
Annual growth in real terms, adjusted for inflation using the Consumer Prices Index including owner occupiers' housing costs (CPIH), was 0.2% for regular pay and 0.3% for total pay, in July to September 2026. When using the Consumer Prices Index excluding owner occupiers' housing costs (CPI), annual growth in real terms for regular pay was 0.4% and for total pay was 0.6%, in July to September 2026.

Our LFS quality update: October 2026 article provides our most up-to-date assessment on the coherence of the latest estimates.

## Real Time Information payrolled employees
Flash estimates should be treated as provisional estimates and are likely to be revised when more data are received next month. We publish information on revisions to payrolled employees monthly. We have also published analysis of recent revisions in our LFS quality update: January 2026 article.
We also publish RTI pay data, which provide a provisional, timelier estimate of median pay. Average weekly earnings and RTI generally trend well for mean total pay. However, there will be differences between the two data sources because of timing and definitional differences.

## Labour Force Survey
Estimates from January to March 2025 include the full effect of the improvements in LFS data collection and sampling methods introduced from January 2024 onwards. Since then, further changes that were introduced to help increase response levels may have an ongoing impact on the survey. These include allocating more interviewers to the LFS from March 2025, which helped drive a recovery of the response levels for the LFS to close to pre-coronavirus pandemic levels in 2026.
Despite the improvement in LFS responses, some volatility remains, particularly for estimates for mid-2023 and throughout 2024, and for granular breakdowns, where sample sizes are smaller. Caution should be taken when drawing conclusions from short-term changes, and we advise users to focus on long term movements in the data.
The dataset size, including imputed households, for the UK LFS was 80,271 individuals in July to September 2026, a decrease of 1.1% in individual interviews compared with April to June 2026. During July to September 2026, mitigative actions for the operational issue drove a recovery in the affected response rates. However, as a consequence of LFS imputation rules, a second-wave impact was observed in July to September 2026, with a slight decrease in overall dataset size. More information can be found in our LFS performance and quality monitoring report: July to September 2026.
We recommend using LFS as part of our suite of labour market indicators, alongside workforce jobs, Claimant Count and Pay As You Earn (PAYE) Real Time Information (RTI) estimates.
The quarterly movements in employment and unemployment in the latest data partly reflect the rotation of the Labour Force Survey (LFS) sample. The group of respondents completing their fifth and final quarter in the survey (June 2026) had lower employment and higher unemployment than both the new group entering the survey (September 2026) and other recent groups of respondents.
As this group left the survey and was replaced, this contributed to the increase in the headline employment rate and the fall in the unemployment rate. The new group entering the survey is more closely aligned with other recent groups, suggesting that the latest movement partly reflects the characteristics of the outgoing group rather than an unusually strong incoming group. More information on understanding LFS single month and wave estimates can be found in our July 2026 quality article.
LFS periods from January to March 2019 are weighted to reflect the size and composition of the UK population, based on 2022 mid-year estimates. For England, Wales and Northern Ireland, they are projected forward using scaling factors from 2021-based national population projections. For Scotland, they are projected forward using scaling factors from 2020-based national population projections. An exercise to reweight LFS data all the way back to 2011, using more up-to-date population estimates and projections, is under way. Because of the scale of this project, revised estimates are expected to be published over a period of around two years, with the first revised estimates expected to be available by March 2027.

## Glossary

### Average weekly earnings
Average weekly earnings (AWE) are calculated using information based on the Monthly Wages and Salaries Survey (MWSS). AWE measures money paid by employers to employees in Great Britain before tax and other deductions from pay. The estimates are not just a measure of pay rises, because they also reflect, for example, changes in the overall structure of the workforce. More high-paid jobs in the economy would have an upward effect on the earnings growth rate.

## Economic inactivity
People not in the labour force who are not in employment but do not meet the internationally accepted definition of unemployment. This is because they have not been seeking work within the last four weeks or they are unable to start work in the next two weeks. The economic inactivity rate is the proportion of people aged between 16 and 64 years who are not in the labour force. The Labour Force Survey (LFS) estimates are official statistics.

## Employment
Employment measures the number of people in paid work or who had a job that they were temporarily away from (for example, because they were on holiday or off sick). This differs from the number of jobs because some people have more than one job. The employment rate is the proportion of people aged between 16 and 64 years who are in employment. The LFS estimates are official statistics.

## Unemployment
Unemployment measures people without a job who have been actively seeking work within the last four weeks and are available to start work within the next two weeks. The unemployment rate is not the proportion of the total population who are unemployed. It is the proportion of the economically active population (people in work and those seeking and available to work) who are unemployed. The LFS estimates are official statistics.

## Claimant Count
The Claimant Count is an official statistic in development that measures the number of people who are receiving a benefit principally for the reason of being unemployed. Currently, the Claimant Count consists of those receiving Jobseeker's Allowance, and Universal Credit claimants in the "searching for work" conditionality group.

## Vacancies
Vacancies are defined as positions for which employers are actively seeking recruits from outside their business or organisation. The estimates are based on the Vacancy Survey. This is a survey of businesses designed to provide estimates of the stock of vacancies across the economy, excluding agriculture, forestry and fishing (a small sector for which the collection of estimates would not be practical).

## Pay As You Earn Real Time Information
These data come from HM Revenue and Customs's (HMRC's) Pay As You Earn (PAYE) Real Time Information (RTI) system. They cover the whole population, rather than a sample of people or companies, and they will allow for more detailed estimates of the population.

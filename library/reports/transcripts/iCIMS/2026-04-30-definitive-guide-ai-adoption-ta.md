## Full report

### Introduction

Artificial intelligence has completely changed the talent acquisition landscape. Over the past two years, organizations have accelerated their investment in AI-driven technology, and vendors across the talent ecosystem have rushed to introduce new capabilities designed to automate tasks, generate content, and improve hiring decisions. As a result, the market is now flooded with research, vendor reports, and commentary on AI adoption.

Despite the volume of information available, most conversations about AI in talent acquisition remain surface level. Many reports focus on the why of AI adoption or provide high-level statistics on use and adoption. Few studies examine the practical reality of how AI is actually being used across the hiring lifecycle, how it can be used, the specific processes it impacts, and the implications for recruiters, hiring managers, and candidates.

This research was designed to move beyond the early conversation about AI. Rather than asking whether organizations are using AI, this report examines how, where, and why AI is being deployed across talent acquisition processes, and what opportunities and risks they must consider. It provides a comprehensive view of AI adoption from sourcing and screening to interviewing, communication, and decision-making.

According to Aptitude Research, 69% of companies report that they are currently using AI in some capacity within talent acquisition, with the highest levels of adoption in screening, candidate communication, and assessments. Yet adoption is behind. Most organizations are using AI in isolated use cases rather than implementing it strategically across the hiring lifecycle. At the same time, Agentic AI is reshaping expectations for what recruiting technology can deliver.

This report takes a deeper look at these shifts. It examines the evolving role of AI across every stage of talent acquisition and explores three critical dimensions of impact:

- **Recruiter Experience** — How AI is changing the day-to-day work of recruiting teams. Tasks AI automates; time savings and reallocation; productivity impact.
- **Hiring Manager Experience** — How AI influences decision-making, collaboration, and evaluation. AI-assisted evaluation; candidate insights; collaboration with recruiters.
- **Candidate Experience** — How AI-driven tools shape the way candidates interact with employers. Screening transparency; engagement at scale; communication and response times.

At the same time, AI adoption introduces new challenges. Organizations must balance efficiency with fairness, automation with oversight, and innovation with governance. Questions around transparency, bias monitoring, compliance, and candidate trust have become central to the conversation. Despite growing adoption, many organizations still lack the governance models needed to ensure responsible use of AI.

The goal of this research is to provide talent leaders with a clear and practical framework for understanding AI in hiring today and for preparing their organizations for the next generation of AI-driven recruiting.

### Research Methodology

**Quantitative Research:** Online survey of TA leaders and practitioners. 412 total respondents. Data collected Q1 2026. Company sizes: 25% at 250–999 employees, 31% at 1,000–5,000, 27% at 5,001–10,000, and 17% at 10,000+.

**Qualitative Research:** In-depth interviews with TA leaders.

### Top Findings

1. **AI adoption in talent acquisition is widespread but still immature.** Most organizations report using AI in some part of the hiring process. However, adoption is typically limited to specific use cases rather than a coordinated strategy across the hiring lifecycle. Only 18% of companies are using AI broadly across TA processes. We also found a wide gap in AI education, as some respondents view automation and AI as the same.

2. **AI is most commonly used in screening, communication, assessments and sourcing.** These stages of the hiring process involve large volumes of candidate data and repetitive tasks, making them natural starting points for AI adoption. Screening is the most common use case, with 58% of companies reporting adoption. 46% of companies are also using AI in sourcing, extending access beyond traditional recruiting roles.

3. **Efficiency remains the primary driver of AI investment.** Organizations continue to adopt AI primarily to reduce manual work, accelerate hiring timelines, and improve recruiter productivity. 50% of companies cite efficiency as their top reason for adopting AI.

4. **Generative AI and Agentic AI is expanding how recruiters communicate and evaluate candidates.** Generative AI and Agentic AI tools are helping recruiters draft outreach messages, summarize candidate information, and generate interview insights, significantly reducing administrative workload. 54% of companies are using AI for candidate communication and engagement.

5. **AI is beginning to influence hiring decisions, not just processes.** While early AI adoption focused on automation, newer tools are providing decision-support insights that help recruiters and hiring managers evaluate candidate qualifications and potential.

6. **Recruiter roles are shifting toward more strategic activities.** As AI automates administrative tasks such as scheduling, resume review, and messaging, recruiters are spending more time on candidate engagement, talent advisory, and workforce planning. Over 60% of organizations report time savings of more than 5 hours per week in screening activities alone.

7. **Hiring managers are becoming direct users of AI-driven hiring tools.** AI-generated candidate summaries, interview insights, and structured interview guidance are helping hiring managers make faster and more consistent hiring decisions.

8. **Candidate expectations are evolving as AI becomes more visible in hiring.** AI-powered communication and chatbots can improve response times and engagement, but organizations must balance automation with personalization to maintain trust. 54% of companies are using AI for candidate communication, increasing exposure to AI-driven interactions.

9. **Governance and transparency remain major challenges.** Many organizations lack clear policies for monitoring bias, ensuring transparency in AI recommendations, and maintaining human oversight in hiring decisions.

10. **The next phase of AI adoption will focus on orchestration of agents.** Rather than individual AI tools supporting isolated tasks, the future of talent acquisition will likely involve networks of AI agents that coordinate sourcing, communication, screening, and scheduling across the hiring process. 46% of companies are already using or planning to use agentic AI to support talent acquisition.

### Defining AI in Talent Acquisition

Before exploring how AI is used across each process of Talent Acquisition, it is important to level set and define AI. AI has become one of the most widely used but misunderstood areas of TA tech. Many talent leaders struggle to distinguish between different types of AI and understand how these technologies should be applied across the hiring process. We found that 58% of TA leaders are not clear about the difference between AI and automation.

For the purposes of this report, AI in talent acquisition should not be viewed as a single technology or standalone product. Instead, AI represents a continuum of capabilities that enable recruiting teams to automate tasks, generate insights, create content, and orchestrate hiring workflows.

Understanding this evolution is critical for organizations evaluating AI or automation technologies and developing strategies for responsible adoption. The evolution framework below provides a clearer picture of how AI operates within modern hiring environments and how organizations can approach adoption strategically. The framework moves from "support the work" toward "do the work":

1. **Automation (Rules & Workflows)** — Automated interview scheduling; workflow triggers within ATS systems; knockout screening questions; candidate status notifications; offer and approval routing. (Automation is not AI unless it uses Machine Learning.)
2. **AI (Prediction & Matching)** — Candidate matching and recommendation engines; resume parsing and candidate ranking; skills inference and talent intelligence; predictive sourcing recommendations; labor market analytics.
3. **Generative AI (Create & Communicate)** — Job description generation; candidate outreach and messaging; interview summaries; recruiter copilots and assistants; candidate engagement chatbots.
4. **Agentic AI (Autonomous Action)** — Autonomous sourcing agents that identify and engage candidates; AI agents that manage interview coordination and scheduling; candidate engagement agents that maintain ongoing communication; workflow orchestration agents that manage hiring pipelines.

**Automation.** Automation represents the earliest stage of intelligence in talent acquisition technology. These systems rely on predefined rules and workflows to execute repetitive tasks without human intervention. Automations typically do not rely on artificial intelligence. Examples include workflow automation and automated notifications sent to candidates at various stages of the process.

**Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning.** AI or Machine Learning requires that a machine make some kind of inference about the next step.

**Generative AI.** Generative AI systems are powered by large language models capable of generating natural language responses, writing text, and synthesizing information across large datasets.

**Agentic AI.** Unlike previous stages, which assist with tasks or generate insights, agentic systems can execute multi-step workflows autonomously while operating within defined human guardrails. Agentic AI systems can coordinate actions across multiple tools and processes, enabling them to perform tasks such as identifying candidates, initiating outreach, scheduling interviews, and updating recruiting systems. These systems do not simply automate individual tasks but they orchestrate activities across the hiring lifecycle.

While still in early stages, this orchestration model has the potential to significantly reshape how recruiting processes are designed. This report found that 46% of companies are using or planning to use Agentic AI to support TA efforts and these companies have more comfort with Agentic AI than with other AI capabilities.

**Figure 2: Use of Agentic AI** — 16% Using; 30% Planning to Use; 27% Not Using; 15% Not Using and Not Comfortable Using; 12% Don't Know.

### The Current State of AI Adoption

Over the past several years, organizations have accelerated their investment in AI tools designed to automate tasks, analyze candidate data, and improve hiring outcomes. At the same time, vendors have embedded AI into nearly every aspect of recruiting systems.

The result is a hiring environment where AI is increasingly present across the talent acquisition lifecycle from sourcing and screening candidates to coordinating interviews and communicating with applicants. Yet while adoption is widespread, maturity remains uneven. Many organizations are still in the early stages of implementation and are learning how to integrate AI effectively into their hiring processes. Only 18% of companies are using AI broadly across processes. Most organizations are still using AI in targeted areas of the hiring process rather than implementing a comprehensive AI strategy across the entire talent acquisition lifecycle.

> "Using AI and using it strategically across processes are two very different things. Companies still treating AI as a collection of point solutions are leaving significant value on the table. The organizations that figure out orchestration across talent acquisition will be the ones setting the standard for what exceptional hiring looks like."
> — Dan Joplin, chief AI officer, iCIMS

**Figure 3: Investment in AI in Talent Acquisition** — Not Using 31%; Using in Specific Use Cases 29%; Piloting 22%; Using Broadly Across TA 18%.

**Where AI Is Being Used Today.** The most common applications of AI in talent acquisition are concentrated in processes that involve large volumes of candidate data or repetitive administrative tasks: resume screening; candidate communication and engagement; assessments; candidate ranking; candidate sourcing and talent search; interview coordination and scheduling; interview summaries and analysis.

We found that screening is the most common use case for 58% of companies.

**Figure 4: Use Cases of AI in Talent Acquisition** — Screening 58%; Communication 54%; Assessments 50%; Sourcing 46%; Interviewing 22%; Other / Write In 4%.

These use cases demonstrate where AI can provide immediate operational benefits. AI systems can analyze thousands of candidate profiles, identify patterns across skills and experience, and automate communication tasks that would otherwise require significant recruiter time.

**AI Is Becoming Embedded in the Talent Acquisition Technology Stack.** Another defining trend in the current state of AI adoption is the shift from standalone AI tools to embedded intelligence within recruiting platforms. Historically, organizations often adopted AI through specialized solutions designed to address a specific challenge, such as sourcing or candidate matching. Today, AI capabilities are increasingly integrated into core systems including applicant tracking systems (ATS), candidate relationship management platforms (CRM), sourcing platforms, interview management systems, and recruitment marketing solutions. This shift reflects broader changes in the talent acquisition technology market. Vendors are moving toward AI-enabled platforms, where intelligence is built directly into recruiting workflows rather than delivered through separate tools.

**Figure 5: Where AI Lives in the TA Tech Stack** — Screening 51%; Sourcing 42%; CRM 36%; Interview Scheduling tools 32%; Assessing 25%; ATS 24%.

**Efficiency Remains the Primary Driver of AI Adoption.** Despite the growing sophistication of AI capabilities, the primary reason organizations adopt AI in talent acquisition remains operational efficiency. 50% stated that efficiency is the top goal and only 28% stated that better decision-making is a top goal. Recruiting teams face increasing pressure to manage larger candidate pools, reduce time-to-hire, and improve hiring outcomes with limited resources. AI helps address these challenges by automating repetitive tasks and enabling recruiters to focus on higher-value activities.

**Figure 6: Time savings Using AI** (by process — Sourcing / Screening / Interviewing / Communication):
- None: 8% / 6% / 10% / 7%
- Less than 1 hour: 15% / 9% / 8% / 14%
- 1-5 hours: 28% / 16% / 23% / 26%
- 5-10 hours: 35% / 38% / 32% / 31%
- More than 10 hours: 14% / 32% / 27% / 22%

Many organizations report that AI technologies enable recruiters to spend less time on administrative tasks such as scheduling interviews, screening resumes, or sending routine communications. Instead, recruiters can allocate more time to strategic activities such as engaging with candidates, advising hiring managers, and building talent pipelines.

**Figure 7: Where Recruiters Will Allocate Time** (select top three) — Engaging and nurturing relationships 80%; Strengthening hiring manager partnership 73%; Strategic talent planning and advising 64%; No meaningful reallocation 17%; Other 8%.

This shift represents one of the most significant impacts of AI adoption in talent acquisition: redefining the role of the recruiter.

**Challenges with AI Adoption.** Many organizations remain unprepared for the broader implications of AI in hiring. Several challenges continue to slow adoption: lack of clear governance frameworks; limited transparency into AI decision making; integration challenges across recruiting systems; concerns about bias and fairness; regulatory uncertainty.

The current state of AI adoption in talent acquisition can best be described as a transition period. Most organizations have moved beyond experimentation and are actively using AI in parts of their hiring processes. However, relatively few have developed a cohesive strategy for how AI should operate across the entire hiring lifecycle.

### AI Across the Talent Acquisition Lifecycle

Each section of this report looks at the use of AI in each process, the opportunities, and the potential risks. Each section also explores different examples of automation, generative AI, and Agentic AI. These sections are organized by the candidate's journey through TA.

### AI in Sourcing

Sourcing has become one of the earliest and most visible areas of AI adoption in talent acquisition. As organizations face increasing competition for talent and manage larger volumes of candidate data across multiple platforms, AI technologies are helping recruiting teams identify, evaluate, and engage potential candidates more efficiently.

Traditional sourcing relied heavily on manual search, recruiter networks, and job board activity. Recruiters spent significant time searching databases, reviewing candidate profiles, and identifying potential matches based on keywords or job titles. While these approaches remain part of modern recruiting strategies, the scale of talent data available today has made manual sourcing increasingly difficult.

AI is transforming sourcing by enabling organizations to analyze large datasets of candidate profiles, skills, and career histories. Rather than relying solely on manual searches, recruiters can use AI-driven recommendations and insights to identify potential candidates and expand their talent pipelines.

**How AI Is Used in Sourcing.** AI supports sourcing through several capabilities that improve how organizations identify and engage with potential candidates. The most common use case is for identifying potential candidates followed by matching.

**Figure 8: Most Common Uses of AI in Sourcing** — Identifying potential candidates 33%; Matching candidates to open roles 31%; Rediscovering existing talent in CRM/ATS 14%; Writing communication with talent 12%; Writing or optimizing job descriptions 7%; AI is not used in sourcing 4%.

A few of the most common applications include:
- **Talent rediscovery:** Many organizations have large internal databases of past applicants, previous candidates, and CRM contacts. AI-powered sourcing tools can analyze these internal databases and identify candidates who may be relevant for new roles, helping organizations maximize the value of existing talent pools.
- **Skills-based sourcing:** Instead of searching for candidates based solely on job titles or keywords, AI models can infer skills from resumes, career histories, and other data sources.
- **Candidate outreach:** These tools can generate personalized messages based on a candidate's background, helping recruiters initiate engagement more quickly while maintaining a level of personalization.

**Opportunities for Talent Acquisition.** The use of AI in sourcing presents several opportunities for organizations seeking to improve the effectiveness of their recruiting strategies. Currently, most organizations are only using AI sourcing recommendations occasionally.

**Figure 9: How often recruiters use AI recommendations for sourcing** — Occasionally 46%; Frequently 34%; Always / default workflow 10%; Never 10%.

One of the most significant opportunities is the ability to expand talent pools or rediscovery of talent. AI systems can analyze large volumes of candidate data and identify potential matches that may not appear through traditional search methods. By identifying candidates with adjacent skills or relevant experience, AI can help organizations uncover talent that might otherwise be overlooked. Another important opportunity is improved recruiter productivity. AI-driven recommendations and talent discovery tools reduce the time recruiters spend manually searching for candidates.

**Risks and Considerations.** Despite the benefits of AI-powered sourcing, organizations must carefully consider the risks associated with algorithmic candidate discovery: reinforcing historical bias (AI models trained on past hiring data may replicate existing bias or limited diversity patterns); lack of transparency (complex algorithms can make it difficult for recruiters to understand how candidates are identified or ranked); over-reliance on AI recommendations (AI should support sourcing, not replace recruiter judgment and relationship building); governance and oversight risks (organizations must monitor models for bias, ensure transparency, and maintain human oversight in sourcing decisions).

*Examples in sourcing* — Automation: automated candidate search filters, resume parsing and database matching, talent rediscovery workflows, automated job alerts and talent pool tagging, workflow triggers for candidate pipeline updates. Generative AI: AI-generated outreach messages, personalized candidate outreach drafts, AI-generated candidate summaries, AI-generated recruiter messaging for passive candidates, AI-assisted candidate research summaries. Agentic AI: agents that identify and engage candidates automatically, agents that continuously build and update talent pipelines, autonomous sourcing across internal and external databases, agents that identify skills-adjacent candidates for roles, agents that monitor talent markets and surface candidates proactively.

#### Case Study: How ATCC Transformed Scientific Hiring with AI-Driven Precision

**About the organization.** ATCC is a global scientific organization dedicated to advancing public health through cutting-edge biological research and innovation, serving as a trusted partner to researchers, public health institutions, and regulated environments worldwide. To sustain this mission, ATCC depends on niche scientific and technical talent capable of operating in tightly controlled, compliance-driven settings while supporting high-stakes research outcomes.

**Results:** 28% cut in time to hire; $2M+ saved in agency & RPO spend; 90% reduction in time to apply; 12x increase in applicant volume; ~90% drop in temp onboarding need.

**The challenge.** Specialized roles required deep technical evaluation, regulatory compliance added complexity at every stage, and delays in hiring slowed critical scientific programs. Traditional, transactional recruiting lacked the intelligence and automation needed to scale efficiently while maintaining quality and compliance.

**The solution.** When ATCC began rethinking its screening process, the team turned to AI to bring greater precision and consistency to hiring. With resume-to-job alignment powered by AI, recruiters gained immediate access to data-driven match scores and insights that guided faster, more confident decisions, especially for highly specialized roles. The team also used AI-generated interview questions custom-built from each job description and intake session to help every hiring manager conduct structured, relevant, and compliant evaluations. Real-time candidate insights within the platform kept the process objective and aligned with ATCC's strict regulatory standards.

**The impact.** Within the first year, the company cut time to hire by 28%, allowing leaders to align top talent to critical research initiatives faster. By eliminating agency and RPO spend, ATCC saved more than $2 million, improving cost-per-hire. On the candidate side, a 90% reduction in time to apply nearly erased incomplete applications, which had previously hovered around 30% (dropping to ~3% after AI). AI-optimized job postings and simplified application flows fueled a 12x increase in applicant volume. The organization reduced its reliance on temporary onboarding support by nearly 90%. The 12-month post-implementation time-to-start index dropped 28%.

**Compliance & regulatory impact.** Connected hiring and workforce data brought new levels of clarity to compliance reporting, streamlining OFCCP-aligned AAP processes, and ensuring every decision met the organization's high regulatory standards.

### AI in Screening

Screening is one of the most widely adopted applications of AI in talent acquisition. As organizations receive increasing volumes of applications for open roles, recruiting teams face significant pressure to quickly identify qualified candidates while maintaining fairness and consistency in the evaluation process.

Traditionally, screening relied on manual resume reviews, recruiter judgment, and basic qualification checks. Recruiters often spent hours reviewing resumes, identifying relevant experience, and determining which candidates should move forward. As applicant volumes increased and hiring timelines accelerated, this manual approach became increasingly difficult to sustain.

AI-powered screening tools can help recruiters prioritize candidates, evaluate qualifications, and reduce the time required to move candidates through early stages. At the same time, screening represents one of the most sensitive areas for AI adoption. Because screening often influences who advances in the hiring process, organizations must ensure that AI is used responsibly, with appropriate oversight and transparency.

**How AI Is Used in Screening.** One of the most common uses of AI in screening is automated screening questions and candidate matching. Most companies are already using resume parsing even if they do not realize they are using it in their ATS. AI models analyze resumes and candidate profiles to identify relevant experience, skills, and qualifications.

**Figure 10: Use Cases for AI in Screening** — Automated screening questions 55%; Candidate matching or fit scoring 52%; Resume parsing and normalization 41%; Ranking or prioritizing candidates 25%; AI is not used in screening 8%.

In some cases, AI is used to support decisions or recommendations during screening. Most organizations still maintain human oversight during screening decisions. AI systems are often used to assist recruiters rather than automatically exclude candidates. Many organizations also evaluate their screening tools regularly to monitor for bias and ensure fairness. We found that 46% of companies say that AI excludes candidates based on rules while 38% say it excludes them based on AI output.

**Figure 11: Are Recruiters Excluding Candidates** — Yes, based on rules and/or knockout questions 46%; Yes, based on AI output 38%; No 12%; Not sure (remainder).

**Opportunities.** Faster evaluation of candidate applications; improved consistency in candidate evaluation; support for skills-based hiring; reduced administrative burden for recruiters; better prioritization of qualified candidates.

**Risks.** Most organizations in this study identified screening as medium risk. Less than 50% of companies regularly check screening for bias.

**Figure 12: Risk for Screening** — Low 8%; Medium 54%; High 38%.

Companies must consider: algorithmic bias (models trained on historical hiring data may replicate existing bias); lack of transparency in screening decisions; over-automation of candidate rejection (automated processes may eliminate qualified candidates without human oversight); compliance and regulatory challenges (regulations require demonstrating fairness, transparency, and auditability); candidate trust and experience concerns.

*Examples in screening* — Automation: knockout screening questions, automated resume scoring, application workflow routing, automated eligibility checks, candidate ranking based on predefined criteria. Generative AI: AI-generated candidate summaries, AI-generated recruiter insights on candidate fit, AI-generated screening notes from candidate profiles, AI-generated summaries of candidate experience, AI-assisted evaluation summaries. Agentic AI: agents that evaluate candidate qualifications and recommend next steps, agents that prioritize and advance candidates through screening stages, agents that evaluate skills and flag strong or weak candidates, agents that recommend interview shortlists based on role requirements, agents that monitor candidate pipelines and update rankings dynamically.

### AI in Interviewing

While interviews have historically relied heavily on human judgment, AI is increasingly being used to support various aspects of the interview process. These technologies help organizations coordinate interviews more efficiently, provide structured insights to hiring teams, and reduce administrative tasks associated with interview management.

AI-driven interview technologies can assist with scheduling, summarizing interview feedback, and supporting more structured evaluations of candidates. These capabilities allow recruiters and hiring managers to focus more on meaningful candidate interactions rather than logistical coordination.

**How AI Is Used in Interviewing.** AI supports several aspects of the interview process including content, intelligence, and scheduling. One of the most widely used applications is interview content generation. AI can guide hiring managers and standardize interview questions. AI-powered scheduling tools can automatically identify available interview times across recruiter, hiring manager, and candidate calendars, reducing back-and-forth communication. Despite being one of the most frustrating areas for recruiting teams, only 32% of companies automate the scheduling aspect of interviewing.

**Figure 13: Use Cases for AI in Interviewing** (select top three) — Interview content or questions 45%; Interview reminders & follow-ups 43%; Automated scheduling 38%; Interview analysis or summaries 30%; Interview transcription 22%; AI is not used 20%.

1-in-2 companies stated that AI in interviewing has significantly reduced recruiter time.

**Opportunities.** Reduced administrative workload; improved consistency in interview evaluations; faster hiring decisions; better collaboration between recruiters and hiring managers; improved recruiter productivity.

**Risks.** Over-automation of the interview process; potential bias in evaluation models; interviewer over-reliance on AI insights; candidate perception concerns; governance and compliance risks. Organizations that integrate AI thoughtfully into the interview process can improve efficiency and consistency while preserving the human elements essential to effective hiring.

*Examples in interviewing* — Automation: automated interview scheduling, interview reminder notifications, calendar coordination workflows, interview workflow tracking, candidate interview confirmations. Generative AI: AI-generated interview summaries, AI-generated interview feedback summaries, AI-generated interview questions, AI-generated candidate comparison summaries, AI-assisted interviewer briefing notes. Agentic AI: agents that coordinate interview scheduling across calendars, agents that compile interviewer feedback and generate evaluations, agents that guide interviewers with structured interview prompts, agents that monitor interview pipelines and recommend decisions, agents that orchestrate interview logistics across recruiting teams.

#### Case Study: Automated Recruiting & Long-Term Candidate Engagement (ATI)

**About the organization.** ATI, one of the largest providers of outpatient physical therapy in the United States, operates hundreds of clinics nationwide and employs approximately 5,000 people. Recruiting for clinical roles such as physical therapists, occupational therapists, and physical therapist assistants presents a unique challenge: many qualified candidates are still students who won't enter the workforce for months or even years.

**Results:** 3x growth in talent database; 400+ day candidate nurture cycle; 17% year-over-year increase in applicants per opening.

**The challenge.** Prior to leveraging automation, ATI's recruiting team spent significant time manually following up with candidates, managing recruiting event lists and coordinating communications.

**The solution.** With automated campaigns and event workflows in place, candidates who attend recruiting events are automatically entered into multi-step engagement journeys tailored to their stage in their academic or professional journey. By automating these communications and administrative tasks, ATI Physical Therapy's recruiters can focus on building relationships with future clinicians and strengthening engagement with hard-to-hire talent, without adding extra headcount.

**The impact.** This long-term candidate engagement strategy delivered measurable results: 3x growth in the company's candidate database, a 400+ day average candidate nurture cycle, and a 17% year-over-year increase in applicants per opening.

### Assessments

AI is increasingly being used to support candidate evaluation and assessment, helping organizations move beyond traditional screening toward more structured and scalable decision-making. While still evolving, this area represents one of the most important shifts in how companies define quality of hire.

**Where AI is Being Used in Assessments.** AI is most commonly applied in pre-hire assessments and skills validation; candidate scoring and ranking based on qualifications and experience; behavioral and skills inference based on candidate data; structured evaluation and consistency across hiring decisions. These capabilities allow organizations to move beyond resume-based screening and introduce more standardized approaches to evaluating candidates at scale. 50% of companies report using AI in assessments and evaluation workflows.

**The Opportunity: Consistency and Better Decision Support.** AI is helping organizations reduce subjectivity in hiring by providing structured insights and recommendations that support both recruiters and hiring managers. Unlike earlier stages of AI adoption focused on efficiency, assessments represent a shift toward decision support. Organizations are using AI to identify patterns across candidate skills and experience, provide consistent evaluation criteria across candidates, support hiring managers with structured insights, and reduce variability in how candidates are assessed. This is especially important as hiring managers become more directly involved in the hiring process. At the same time, AI is not replacing decision-making. It is augmenting it. 26% of companies report using AI specifically to support decision-making, while 33% use it for both efficiency and decision support.

**The Risk: Bias, Transparency, and Over-Reliance.** Assessments represent one of the highest-risk areas for AI adoption. As organizations use AI to evaluate candidates, questions around fairness, bias, and transparency become more critical. Key challenges include: lack of clarity on how candidates are scored or ranked; risk of bias in training data or evaluation models; over-reliance on AI recommendations without human oversight; limited transparency with candidates on how AI is used. These concerns are amplified by a broader lack of understanding of AI itself: 58% of TA leaders report they are not clear on the difference between AI and automation.

**The Impact on the Hiring Experience.** AI-driven assessments are changing how both recruiters and hiring managers evaluate candidates. For recruiters, this means less time spent manually reviewing candidates, more reliance on AI-generated insights and recommendations, and greater focus on validating and interpreting AI outputs. For hiring managers, this means more direct access to candidate insights, increased participation in evaluation and decision-making, and faster and more consistent hiring decisions. At the same time, candidate expectations are evolving: candidates are becoming more aware of AI in hiring and expect transparency in how they are being evaluated, fair and unbiased assessment processes, and the ability to understand or challenge outcomes.

### AI in Candidate Communication

Communication is one of the most visible and widely adopted uses of AI in talent acquisition. Throughout the hiring process, recruiters must manage a high volume of interactions with candidates, including responding to inquiries, scheduling interviews, providing updates, and maintaining engagement with talent pipelines.

Historically, candidate communication has been time-consuming and inconsistent. Recruiters often rely on email or manual messaging, which can lead to delays in response times and gaps in candidate engagement. As candidate expectations for faster and more personalized communication have increased, organizations are turning to AI to help scale communication while maintaining a positive candidate experience.

AI-powered communication tools enable recruiters to automate routine interactions, respond to candidate inquiries more quickly, and maintain engagement with larger talent pools. These technologies can support both recruiters and candidates by improving response times and providing more consistent communication throughout the hiring process.

**How AI Is Used in Candidate Communication.** One common use is automated messaging and communication workflows. AI systems can send status updates, reminders, and interview confirmations to candidates throughout the hiring process. AI-powered chatbots and conversational assistants are also increasingly used to respond to candidate inquiries, answering common questions about job roles, application status, or hiring timelines. Generative AI is also being used to support message creation and personalization. Despite the growing use of AI communication tools, recruiters still play an important role in reviewing and managing communications; in many organizations, recruiters regularly override or edit AI-generated messages to ensure accuracy and personalization. Only 1 in 3 companies are very transparent with candidates about where AI is used in the process.

**Figure 14: How AI Supports Candidate Communication** — Automated email or SMS responses 55%; Chatbots 42%; Personalized messaging 42%; Status updates 35%; AI is not used 14%.

**Opportunities.** Faster response times for candidates; improved candidate engagement (19% of companies do not ask candidates for feedback); scalable communication across large talent pools; reduced administrative workload for recruiters; more consistent candidate experience.

**Risks.** Loss of personalization; candidate trust concerns; inaccurate or inappropriate messaging; limited transparency about AI use; over-reliance on automation. Organizations that use AI to augment, not replace, human communication will be better positioned to improve both recruiter productivity and candidate experience.

*Examples in communication* — Automation: automated interview reminders, application status notifications, automated candidate follow-ups, interview scheduling confirmations, candidate workflow messaging. Generative AI: AI-generated candidate messages, AI-generated recruiter outreach emails, AI-generated job descriptions for outreach, AI-generated responses to candidate inquiries, AI-assisted communication templates. Agentic AI: AI agents that manage candidate communication throughout the hiring process, agents that respond to candidate questions and provide updates, agents that nurture passive candidates over time, agents that manage candidate scheduling and engagement, agents that maintain ongoing engagement with talent pools.

### AI and the Recruiter Experience

Recruiters are among the primary users of artificial intelligence in talent acquisition. As recruiting teams face increasing pressure to manage larger candidate pipelines while improving quality of hire and candidate experience, AI technologies are helping recruiters automate repetitive tasks and focus more on strategic activities.

When asked how AI is improving the recruiter experience, companies cited faster decision-making, more time to engage candidates, and less administrative tasks as the top benefits.

**Figure 15: How has AI Impacted the Recruiter Experience** — Faster decision-making 50%; More time engaging candidates 48%; Less administrative work 25%; No meaningful change 15%.

One of the most common applications is candidate discovery and sourcing recommendations. AI is also widely used to support resume screening and candidate prioritization. Generative AI tools assist recruiters with communication and content creation, including drafting candidate outreach messages, summarizing candidate profiles, and generating interview notes. AI is also increasingly used for interview coordination and scheduling. Together, these capabilities allow recruiters to spend less time on administrative tasks and more time focusing on candidate relationships and hiring strategy.

Recruiters want to be able to step in at any point in the process. When AI conflicts arise, 58% of companies state that recruiter judgment wins.

**Figure 16: Where AI Conflicts with the Recruiter Experience** — Recruiter judgment 58%; AI recommendation 35%; Depends on situation 15%.

### AI and the Hiring Manager Experience

Hiring managers play a critical role in the hiring process, yet historically they have had limited access to recruiting technologies. Most hiring managers rely on recruiters to provide candidate insights, coordinate interviews, and guide hiring decisions.

As AI capabilities become more integrated into recruiting platforms, hiring managers are increasingly interacting directly with AI tools that support candidate evaluation and decision-making. These technologies are designed to help hiring managers review candidate information more efficiently, prepare for interviews, and collaborate more effectively with recruiting teams. AI-driven insights can improve hiring manager decision-making by providing structured data and summaries about candidates.

**How AI Is Used by Hiring Managers.** One common use is candidate evaluation summaries. AI tools can generate summaries of candidate resumes, interview feedback, and assessment results. AI is also used to support interview preparation, generating interview questions or structured interview guides based on role requirements. Some AI tools provide candidate comparison insights, and AI supports interview feedback analysis by consolidating insights from multiple interviewers.

**How AI Is Used by Hiring Managers (chart)** — Reviewing AI-generated candidate matches or shortlists 52%; Reviewing AI-generated interview insights or summaries 45%; Interview scheduling or coordination 40%; Using AI-suggested interview questions 33%; Hiring managers do not directly interact with AI 18%.

### AI and the Candidate Experience

Artificial intelligence is also shaping how candidates interact with employers throughout the hiring process. From job discovery to interview scheduling, candidates are increasingly engaging with AI-powered tools during their interactions with recruiting teams.

Organizations are using AI to provide faster responses to candidate inquiries, automate communication, and maintain engagement with candidates across multiple stages. These technologies can help create a more responsive and scalable candidate experience. At the same time, AI introduces new considerations around transparency and trust. Candidates may not always know when they are interacting with automated systems, and concerns about fairness and transparency can influence how candidates perceive AI-driven hiring processes. As AI becomes more common in recruiting, organizations must ensure that these technologies enhance the candidate experience rather than diminish it.

**How AI Is Used in Candidate Interactions** — Chatbots for questions 42%; Automated scheduling 38%; AI-driven job matching 35%; AI-generated communications 26%; Candidates do not interact with AI 19%.

One of the most common uses is automated communication and chatbots that respond to candidate inquiries and provide information about job opportunities or application status. AI is also used to support interview scheduling and coordination, allowing candidates to quickly select interview times without back-and-forth communication. Generative AI tools may also assist with personalized messaging. Some organizations also use AI to provide candidate insights and feedback, such as summaries of next steps or reminders about upcoming hiring stages.

### Governance & Ownership

Nearly half of organizations using AI in TA have no governance framework in place. This means decisions about fairness, compliance, and candidate trust are being left to chance, or to vendors. For TA leaders, this is a red flag. Without policies, recruiters lack clarity on what tools are allowed, how results should be interpreted, and who is ultimately accountable.

**AI Governance Framework Status** — No Framework 45% (no AI policies or oversight structure); In Development 35% (building policies not yet operational); Fully Established 20% (clear governance and human oversight).

Companies with governance frameworks in place are significantly more likely to report higher trust among recruiters and better candidate feedback. Companies without governance often struggle with adoption because recruiters feel uncertain about when and how to use AI.

### Bias & Fairness

Only 15% of employers are "very confident" that AI reduces bias. That leaves 85% either uncertain or unconvinced. Bias is also the top risk recruiters cite when using AI, ahead of compliance and candidate trust. This reflects a critical reality: AI may help reduce human bias, but if trained on flawed data, it can scale inequity faster than humans ever could.

**Confidence in Bias Reduction** — Somewhat confident 40%; Neutral 25%; Not confident 20%; Very confident 15%.

Employers who prioritize skills-based models report significantly more confidence in bias reduction, because recommendations are tied to transparent, job-relevant attributes. Employers using opaque "black box" AI models report the lowest confidence, and recruiters in those companies are more likely to override or ignore AI outputs.

We found that 82% of companies stated that transparency and explainability is important. Yet only 10% always receive transparency, a 72 percentage-point gap. Recruiters overwhelmingly say transparency is essential, but most do not get it. The majority report that AI explanations are only sometimes or rarely provided. This leaves recruiters stuck: they are expected to rely on tools they cannot fully understand.

**How important is transparency?** — Extremely important 50%; Very important 32%; Somewhat important 13%; Not important 5%.

**Frequency receiving transparency** — Always 10%; Often 25%; Sometimes 40%; Rarely 20%; Never 5%.

### Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is fundamentally reshaping how organizations attract, evaluate, and hire talent. What began as simple automation of recruiting workflows has evolved into a broad ecosystem of technologies capable of generating insights, assisting with decision-making, and orchestrating hiring activities across the talent acquisition lifecycle.

This research demonstrates that AI adoption is accelerating across sourcing, screening, candidate communication, and interviewing. Organizations are using AI to manage increasing candidate volumes, improve recruiter productivity, and create more responsive hiring processes.

However, adoption remains uneven. Most organizations are still implementing AI in targeted areas of the hiring process rather than developing a comprehensive strategy for integrating AI across the hiring lifecycle. As a result, many companies are experiencing both the opportunities and challenges of early-stage AI adoption.

AI technologies can significantly improve recruiting efficiency and provide valuable insights into candidate skills and potential. Yet these systems must be implemented carefully to ensure transparency, fairness, and trust in hiring decisions. Governance, human oversight, and clear policies around AI use will become increasingly important as AI capabilities continue to evolve.

Looking forward, the next phase of AI innovation in talent acquisition will likely focus on agentic systems and workflow orchestration. Instead of individual AI tools supporting isolated tasks, organizations will begin to deploy networks of AI agents that collaborate across sourcing, screening, communication, and interview coordination.

At the same time, the role of the recruiter will continue to evolve. As AI takes on more administrative and analytical work, recruiters will increasingly focus on strategic responsibilities such as candidate engagement, hiring manager advisory, and workforce planning.

The future of talent acquisition will not be defined by AI replacing recruiters, but by humans and intelligent systems working together to improve how organizations identify and hire talent. Organizations that successfully combine technological innovation with responsible governance and strong human judgment will be best positioned to realize the full potential of AI in hiring.

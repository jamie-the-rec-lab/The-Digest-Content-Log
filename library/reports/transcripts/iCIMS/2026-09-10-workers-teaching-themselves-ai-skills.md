## Full report

**The big picture.** "Last month, demand was accelerating on both sides of the Atlantic, and the labor market answered with strain instead of speed. Demand has now turned, and the strain has not gone anywhere. In the U.S., job openings are no longer surging ahead of hiring capacity, growth is decelerating and hiring is slowing even faster. The gap between the two has not closed. It is widening, just from a cooler starting point. EMEA is shifting faster. Openings, applications and hires all declined this month, with hires posting the sharpest single-month drop either region has seen all year."

**US market update.** "August continued a pattern that has been building since June. Job openings finished 13% above the August 2025 baseline... Month-over-month growth for job openings fell from 7% in June to 4% in July to just 1% in August." Hires "ended the month at a 2% increase above the August 2025 baseline and posted their first back-to-back monthly decline of the year, down 1% month-over-month in August after a 3% drop in July." Applications "remained 1% below the August 2025 baseline but are on their third consecutive month of gains." iCIMS strategy note: "The hiring drop this month points away from a sourcing problem and toward a conversion one... Recruiting teams should audit screen-to-interview and interview-to-offer conversion before defaulting to a top-of-funnel fix."

**Time to fill.** "The gap between hirings and openings is affecting time to fill as it slowly increases to an average of 40 days over the last two months." Trent Cotton, Head of Talent Insights, ICIMS: "This is what a constrained talent market looks like. Demand is rising, supply is flat and the only lever left is how well you execute inside your own process. With only 30 candidates per role, every breakdown in your process is costing you real hires."

**EMEA market update.** "Job openings in EMEA finished August at 21% above the August 2025 baseline, but growth has turned negative for two straight months, down 3% month-over-month in July and 5% in August after peaking in March." Hires "fell 20 points in a single month, the largest one-month move in either region this year, pulling the cumulative figure down to just 1% above baseline." iCIMS flags this may be seasonal: "August is peak vacation season across much of EMEA." Applications "ended the month at 3% above the August 2025 baseline... down 3% month-over-month in June, flat in July and down 7% in August."

Cotton, on the EMEA candidate pool: "When the pool was bigger, a slow process cost you good candidates. Now, it costs you the offer entirely. Months of consecutive decline in EMEA means it is worth checking whether your process has sped up to match or stayed the same while the market moved."

**Report focus: the AI skills race.** "This report draws on two data partners... Lightcast's job postings and skills data shows where and how fast AI requirements are spreading across occupations and industries. ICIMS commissioned a survey in August 2026 of 1,000 U.S. adults to understand how they are responding to the AI skills race." Cotton: "I'll admit, I thought skills-based hiring was hype. This data changed my mind. Workers are outpacing employer training. Job postings are outpacing both. That is the market talking, and I trust what the numbers are telling us. This is a topic every practitioner should have a plan for today. The pressure for organizations to win the AI race will only intensify the need for an AI-ready workforce plan."

**Six job titles every market is fighting over.** "AI-related job postings account for only a small share of overall hiring demand: 4% of job postings in the U.S., 2.7% in the U.K. and 1.2% in France." Six occupations had the highest AI-skill-requirement share across all four regions: generative AI engineer, NLP engineer, machine learning engineer, AI engineer, deep learning engineer, and data scientist. "'Data Scientist' is the exception in this group. Between 69% and 79% of Data Scientist job postings ask for an AI skill, depending on the region," leaving 20-30% of Data Scientist postings with no AI requirement — unlike the other five roles, where "nearly every posting... already carries the AI skill requirement."

**Beyond the core occupations.** Roles with high regional (not global) AI-skill penetration: technology brand ambassador (60% in France, 59% Middle East, absent from US/UK top 10); robotics software engineer (85% US, 79% UK, absent from France/Middle East top 10); UX designer (50% in France); transportation security officer (67% in the US, "3,532 AI-related postings out of 5,287" — the largest volume of any role in this group); computational biologist (70% UK, 54% France).

**Regional and sector view.** "Finance leads in three of the four regions (U.S., U.K. and Middle East) with the highest share of roles requiring AI skills, with the U.K. being the highest... Manufacturing came in second in those same three regions... France is the exception to both patterns, with manufacturing leading there and finance sitting second." By skill-reshuffling category: "Healthcare changed most. It has the largest share of Newly Prominent skills and the smallest Stable/Core... Manufacturing changed least, holding the largest amount of Stable/Core skills... Healthcare also had the lowest AI hiring rate of the three sectors."

**Self-teaching outpaces employer training.** "According to our survey of 1,000 job seekers, just under half the U.S. adults (47%) has taken steps to build AI skills in the past six months. Last year that number was 41%. Another 21% have not started but plan to." "Self-teaching for AI skills rose from 22% to 30% in a single year. Employer-provided training for AI held at roughly one in six, moving from 19% to 17%." Among those working full- or part-time: "Thirty-four percent taught themselves versus 24% who received employer-provided training." "Forty-five percent of job seekers say they see GenAI skills listed as a requirement in postings for roles they would consider." Confidence: "Three in five (60%) feel ready to adapt to AI at work, up from 54% a year ago, and the share who feel unprepared fell from 20% to 14%." "Sixty-four percent spent none of their own money doing it." At the other end: "Thirty-two percent of job seekers have not built AI skills and do not plan to. Twenty-six percent of job seekers say they have not considered how AI will affect their job at all."

Strategy recommendations from iCIMS: "Give credit for self-teaching... Fifty-one percent say they currently have GenAI skills or are actively building them, and 57% say they are at least somewhat confident they could meet an AI requirement today." "Separate day-one skills from 90-day skills... Thirty-two percent of adults expect an AI skill to lose its value within a year, and another 33% are not sure how long it will last." "Treat AI training as a recruiting offer... Forty-two percent say a company offering AI training would be more attractive than a similar employer that did not, 28% would favor the company outright and another 14% would even accept lower pay for it."

**The biggest AI skill most workers have is familiarity.** "Fifty-five percent of job seekers say GenAI skills appear in postings they would consider at least occasionally: 21% frequently, 34% occasionally. Only 15% say they never see it." "Two-thirds of workers (67%) say they are at least somewhat confident they could meet an AI requirement today," but "that 67% splits nearly two to one toward the soft end: 42% are 'somewhat confident, with basic exposure,' versus 25% who say they are 'very confident' and already have the skills." "Sixty-one percent report proficiency with a GenAI tool like ChatGPT, Copilot, or Gemini. Every other skill sits two to three times smaller: data analysis with AI tools (30%), prompt engineering (18%), AI ethics or governance (17%) and machine learning or model development (17%)... More than a quarter of workers (27%) report none of these skills at all, even after removing everyone who is not currently working."

iCIMS's framing: "Exposure to AI requirements is broad. Confidence is real but shallow. And the skill behind that confidence is almost entirely one generalist tool, not the specialized skills (prompt engineering, model development, AI governance) that actually define the AI roles employers are hiring for... Training budgets should move past the entry-level tool skill almost everyone already has and start building the specialized skills that are still scarce."

### The big picture

The roles are still open, but the U.S. is running low on applicants and EMEA is running low on hires.

In the U.S., hiring and job applications both slipped below last October’s level while job openings held steady. Employers still have open roles to fill, and fewer candidates are applying for them.

EMEA is telling a different story. Job applications and job openings are both above last year, yet hires remain well below. Candidates are applying, and hiring has not followed.

This month's report also spotlights research from the 2026 Talent Transformation Yearbook. ICIMS partnered with analyst firm Kyle & Co. on two surveys: 1) 1,000 U.S. job seekers and 2) 500 HR and talent leaders to find out what they bought, what is running and what separates strong hiring teams from the rest.

This report provides baseline of data you need to understand what happened this year and what to prepare for 2027.

TOP FINDINGS
- U.S. hiring declined in October by 5 points, coming in -1% below the October 2025 baseline as job applications continue to decline.
- EMEA applications dropped to 9% above the baseline as the gap between job openings and hiring grows.
- 51% of job seekers encountered a multistep AI-led hiring process with little or no direct recruiter or hiring-manager interaction.

### U.S. hires and job applications slip below last year as job openings hold steady

US PLATFORM INDICATORS | Job openings, applications and hires | October 2025–October 2026 (chart, not captured)

ICIMS INSIGHTS QUICK TAKE: Fall has brought a chill to hiring and job applications in the U.S. while job openings hold steady, leaving the widest gap between open roles and hires since January.

MARKET UPDATE

The summer heat has given way to a fall chill. ICIMS platform data for October showed hiring and job applications falling below last October’s level while job openings held steady. In September, hiring landed at 5% above the October 2025 baseline. Now one month later in October, it dropped to 1% below, its first dip under that mark since February. Job applications also fell from 3% above the October 2025 baseline to 3% below.

Job openings sat 7% above last October for the fourth straight month. That leaves an eight-point gap between job openings (+7%) and hires (-1%), the widest since January. With job applications down and job openings steady, applicants per opening sit below the baseline.

Indeed Hiring Lab data points the same way. Job postings on Indeed are about 3% above this time last year, though brand-new postings have drifted sideways. Employers keep posting roles, and hiring has not picked up to match.

If job applications keep slipping while job openings hold, hiring could stay under pressure through the holiday season.

STRATEGY

October’s data points to a candidate supply problem. Job openings are steady, fewer people are applying and fewer applicants are becoming hires, so recruiting leaders have two moves to consider.

- The first priority is speed to response. When job applications are down, each candidate is harder to replace. In an ICIMS survey of 1,000 job seekers, 40% said the wait for a hiring decision was worse than expected. Review how quickly applicants hear back after applying and after each interview, and set a response target for the roles that must be filled before the holidays.
- The second priority is the talent you already know. Past applicants, silver medalists and internal candidates are people who have already shown interest, and reaching out to them costs less than new sourcing. Start with the roles that have been open longest, since they are the most likely to go unfilled while applications stay soft.

Showing leadership how quickly candidates move and how many known candidates are within reach makes the case for where to spend limited recruiting capacity.

### EMEA hires fall behind demand as job application and job openings grow

EMEA PLATFORM INDICATORS | Job openings, applications and hires | October 2025–October 2026 (chart, not captured)

ICIMS INSIGHTS QUICK TAKE: Hiring activity picks up as the summer months end. Is this signs of seasonality or a recovery?

MARKET UPDATE

The summer swings in EMEA hiring have settled, and what remains is a hiring gap. ICIMS platform data for October showed job applications rose 9% above last October and job openings 2% above, while hires sat 18% below. Job openings were back above last year’s level for the first time since March.

Hires have ran below last October’s level every month since. August was the low point, with hires 25% below the baseline, job openings 19% below and applications 9% below. In the following month, September, job applications jumped up to 14% above the baseline, yet hires recovered only to 17% below.

That leaves job applications 27 points above hires and job openings 20 points above hires. More candidates are applying and more roles are open, yet fewer are being filled. The U.S. shows the opposite, with applications below last year.

Eurostat data shows more people looking for work across the euro area. The unemployment rate held at 6.5% in September, with about 420,000 more people out of work than a year earlier.

If job openings keep rising while hires lag, the gap between open roles posted and roles filled could widen into the new year.

STRATEGY

October’s data points to a conversion problem. Job applications and job openings are both above last year, yet hires are down sharply, so recruiting leaders have two priorities.

- The first is to find where candidates stall. Look at average days in stage by role family, starting with the roles that have strong application volume and few hires. Long waits at screening, interviews or approvals are the places to fix first.
- The second is to keep candidates informed while they wait. In an ICIMS survey of 1,000 job seekers, 38% of Gen Z job seekers say not hearing back is their biggest frustration. Automated status updates keep applicants engaged while the process catches up, and they protect your employer brand with candidates who are not hired.

With demand building across EMEA, the teams that fix their slowest stage first could convert the next wave of job openings into hires faster than their peers.

### CHROs and heads of talent rate their AI alike and surprise researchers

Kyle & Co. expected the 45% of leaders who call their AI a success to split by job title. Every respondent answered the same question: “Overall, how would you rate the success of your organization’s AI implementation over the past two years?” The survey reached 100 CHROs and chief people officers and 400 heads of talent and talent acquisition. The team assumed executives and operators would answer differently, as they did in past HR technology waves.

What was the top answer?
Highly successful, with clear and measurable business impact. 44% of CHROs and 46% of heads of talent chose it, two points apart. Moderately successful came next at 46% overall, which puts nine in ten leaders on the success side. No one in either group called their AI largely unsuccessful or said it was too early to tell.

Where the group part ways
The differences show up below the top answer. 52% of CHROs say moderately successful, against 44% of heads of talent. Mixed results drew 4% of CHROs and 10% of heads of talent. Recruiters closest to the work report a few more rough edges, yet both groups land in the same place.

Where the group agrees
The match goes beyond the success rating. The two groups sit one point apart on how much of the AI they bought is in production. They also give the same answers on readiness, change management, their own hiring performance, what blocks progress and what drives investment. Across the survey, pressure from the CEO or board (22%), cost targets (21%), AI arriving in tools already owned (21%) and competitive pressure (20%) all draw similar shares.

The takeaway
The leadership gap many expected did not appear. Executives and recruiters describe the same year, which suggests the harder question for 2027 is what is running.

The average company uses about 52% of the AI it bought, and that number could say more about results than the success rating does.

If you are looking for a successful AI strategy in 2027, it may start with an audit of the tools you already have access to.

### Finished groundwork lifts AI success

Every school year comes with homework, and the Class of 2026 turned in uneven work. Kyle & Co. asked 500 HR and talent leaders about five groundwork steps for running AI, from training the team to preparing managers. The leaders who finished the most steps also reported the strongest hiring results.

What are the five steps?
- Step 1: AI training for the HR team
- Step 2: Documented ownership of the process and data behind AI-supported work
- Step 3: Contract terms covering AI audits and accountability
- Step 4: Manager training on workflows AI has changed
- Step 5: Investment in internal mobility.

Across the Class, each step is finished at 38% to 43% of companies and in progress at another 43% to 50%. Contract terms lead at 43% finished and AI training trails at 38%. Internal mobility has the most momentum, with 88% finished or started.

What did finishing them change?
Companies that finished none or one step call their AI highly successful 25% of the time. At two steps it is 44%, and at three or more it is 65%. Each step adds roughly 20 points to the success rating and about 30 points to the odds of landing in the Upper Classmen tier. Companies with contract terms done rate their AI highly successful at 54%, against 39% of those without. AI training shows the sharpest split, finished by 57% of Upper Classmen and 17% of Freshmen.

### AI through the eyes of the candidate

Job seekers face more AI and longer waits as employers stay quiet about how it is used.

ICIMS asked 1,000 U.S. job seekers what hiring felt like this year, and the answers describe a process that is more automated, slower than promised and more focused on skills.

- Job seekers are moving through a hiring process increasingly run by AI, and many say the wait for an answer is longer than they expected, according to new ICIMS research on U.S. candidates.
- More than half of job seekers (51%) went through a multistep AI-led process with little or no direct contact with a recruiter or hiring manager. Four in ten say the time between applying and hearing a decision was worse than expected, while about one in four say it was better. AI is often sold as a way to speed up hiring, and candidates are not yet feeling it.
- Employers have also said little about how the technology is used. Among job seekers who believe AI evaluated their application, only 32% say the employer clearly disclosed it. Another 41% suspected AI was in use and heard nothing.
- Skills may be the bright spot. More than half of job seekers (56%) have received a job inquiry or recommendation based on their skills instead of their job title, and 41% want employers to prioritize skills over titles or degrees in 2027.

The findings suggest AI can help when employers explain it and use it to place the right candidate in the right role faster, instead of simply cutting cost or headcount. Those that pair automation with clear communication could earn candidate trust heading into 2027.

**When to use this:** you want a fast read on what OpenAI's new GPT-6 "Astra" model can actually do with computer use, beyond the hype headlines.

**Big idea:** Astra's real leap is computer use, meaning it can directly operate complex professional software like a person would, not just chat or browse, which unlocks creative and technical work that previously needed real skill in the tool itself.

**Frameworks & plays:**
- Astra can drive full creative applications end to end via computer use: hosts cite people using it to build 3D scenes and rig characters in Blender, edit footage in Da Vinci Resolve, draw in MS Paint, and compose in Logic or GarageBand, all from natural-language instructions rather than manual tool operation.
- A community member built an MCP connecting an Astra agent directly to Blender, which the hosts say cuts scene generation time "from like 15 minutes down to like 3 minutes," a concrete sign of how fast the tooling around a new frontier model can mature.
- Scott Stevenson's "Super Astro" project shows Astra editing classic Nintendo game ROMs live from plain-language prompts, for example changing what happens when Mario grabs a coin, which points to real-time game modding as a near-term use case.
- Gavin built and shipped a live product in two days using Astra: "King of the Prompts" (kingoftheprompts.com), a real-time head-to-head AI video prompt battle using the MiniMax/Fal H3 Max video model, complete with a 5-second ad break generated in the same model between rounds.

**Data & benchmarks:**
- Running King of the Prompts costs roughly $75 a day once a promotional credit runs out, which Gavin flags as expensive enough that a pay-per-generation model would need to charge around $15 for about 40 generations to break even.
- Both hosts describe repeatedly hitting usage limits on OpenAI's $200-a-month plan within a single weekend of heavy Astra use.

**Contrarian take:**
- The hosts flag a live credit dispute as a preview of a coming problem: NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic's Levent Alpöge made progress on a 200-year-old fluid dynamics problem using AI models, and Buckmaster has alleged OpenAI then produced a matching proof internally and denied training on his prompts specifically while declining to answer whether it trains on user prompts more broadly, raising the question of who owns an idea when the lab providing the tool can see what a researcher is close to solving.
